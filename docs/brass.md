# Brass

A module that plays one MPX part on a physically modelled brass instrument, starting with the tenor trombone. The lips, the air column, the bell and the player are all inside it, as the string, the bow, the body and the bowing are inside the bowed-string modules.

Names: not fixed. One module, provisionally mpxTrombone, while the engine is unproven; the trumpet, flugelhorn and horn follow as their own slugs on the same engine.

Status: phases 1 to 5 built — the benchmark (`research/brass/trombone-benchmark.md`, `make brassref`); the air column, as a grid and as delay lines with grids at the ends, both within 3 cents of the calculation in frequency (`make brassbore`); the lips, Adachi and Sato's hinged block shutting from the corners, playing on the delay lines (`make brasslips`); brassiness, the wave to the bell steepening along the slide, outside the loop that sets up the oscillation (`make brasslips ARGS=brass`); and the sound that leaves the bell, Levine and Schwinger's radiation to a listener 20 degrees off its axis, set beside the Iowa recordings (`make brassradiation`, `make brasssound`). No player yet. The literature is surveyed in `research/brass/` (five files: the physics and methods common to brass, the trumpet and flugelhorn, the trombone, the French horn, and the player with existing implementations).

## Purpose

To push the state of the art, not to match it. Two kinds of brass model exist today and neither does what this one aims to.

Research models are physically complete and not playable. The Edinburgh finite-difference brass environment matches measured input impedances of a trumpet, a trombone and a horn to about 1 per cent in frequency (Bilbao and Chick 2013), and has a score format with physical units, but it runs offline or near real time, is driven by breakpoint files, and leaves out the slide and nonlinear propagation (Harrison et al. 2015). The finite-volume models that do include nonlinear propagation take minutes per second of sound (Berjamin et al. 2017).

Commercial models are playable and simplified. SWAM Solo Brass is a physical model with idealised straight pipes and spectral correction controls; Sample Modeling's brass is recorded material made continuous by a modelling layer, and owners commonly rate it the more convincing of the two; Arturia's Brass, built with IRCAM and now discontinued, made its physical model playable by finding control parameters for every note with an offline optimisation. All of them are played from MIDI as it arrives, so none can plan.

What nobody has built is a physically complete model, run in real time, played by a performer who reads the score ahead. MPX has the score ahead: notes are announced on the cable four and a half seconds before they sound, and the bowed strings already plan their bowing from that and rehearse difficult starts on a copy of the instrument. With the strings the largest gains came from the player — look-ahead bowing, rehearsed starts, planned bow changes — and listeners judge brass realism mostly on attacks, slurs and changes of dynamics (research/brass/player-and-implementations.md, B5), which are the player's.

Where this is least certain: matching the timbre of recorded instruments with physics alone, which is where pure physical models are most often called synthetic; the lips, the least certain physics in the instrument; and the absence of published measurements for some of what a player does (vibrato rates, tonguing speeds, trombone blowing pressures across the dynamics).

## Why the trombone first

The best open data of the four instruments: a table of every tube section and the bell's shape (Smyth and Scott 2011, open access), the measured input impedance of a named instrument (Bilbao and Chick 2013, Conn 8H), a raw impedance measurement file (UNSW), measurements of ten trombonists playing (Boutin et al. 2015, 2020), and anechoic recordings at three dynamics (University of Iowa). A real-time finite-difference trombone exists as C++ source under the MIT licence (Willemsen et al. 2021; github.com/SilvinWillemsen/cppBrass, LICENSE in the Trombone folder), to read and to measure against.

The simplest air column: mostly cylindrical, which a waveguide represents exactly, and no valves. The slide is a change of length, which also gives glissando.

The best-understood brassiness: shock waves were first measured in a trombone (Hirschberg, Gilbert, Msallam and Wijnands 1996), and the same work showed that the nonlinearity can be left out of the loop that sets up the oscillation.

The trumpet comes second (valves; the best pressure and spectrum data; a fully published real-time algorithm, D'haes and Rodet 2002), the flugelhorn third (the trumpet's valves and lips on a conical bore for which no profile is published), and the horn last (no open bore of a modern double horn, the hand in the bell, a long conical bore that is the waveguide's weak spot, and high notes on closely spaced resonances).

## The instrument in outline

A brass instrument is a self-sustained oscillator in the same form as the bowed string: a nonlinear exciter coupled to a nearly linear delay-based resonator (McIntyre, Schumacher and Woodhouse 1983).

**The lips** are a valve that the mouth pressure pushes open: in the simplest model one mass on a spring with damping, driven by the pressure difference across the lips over an effective area, with Bernoulli flow through the opening and a swept flow from the moving lip surface (Adachi and Sato 1995; Harrison et al. 2015). They sound above their own mechanical resonance. Measured lips have more than one resonance and change from outward-striking to sideways-striking in the high register (Yoshikawa 1995; Newton et al. 2008).

**The air column** — mouthpiece, leadpipe, slide, gooseneck, tuning slide and bell — is described by its input impedance. On the Conn 8H in first position the measured maxima are 38.3, 113.2, 174.0, 233.2, 296.0, 350.9 and 410.1 Hz: peaks 2 to 7 lie near a harmonic series on about 58.5 Hz, and the first, at 38 Hz, does not belong to it. The pedal note B flat 1 has no peak under it and is held by the higher peaks at its harmonics. The mouthpiece raises the impedance across the playing range.

**The bell** sets a cutoff near 1000 Hz on the trombone: below it most of the wave is reflected and sets the pitch; above it the wave leaves. What is heard is the bell's high-pass transfer of the pressure inside, with maxima and minima below the cutoff and a nearly flat response above it on axis (Beauchamp 2012).

**Brassiness** is propagation, not the lips. At fortissimo the wave leaving the mouthpiece steepens along the narrow cylindrical slide until it is nearly a shock at the slide's end, and the radiated sound gains strong high harmonics (Hirschberg et al. 1996; Gilbert and Petiot 1997). The lips' open area hardly changes when the instrument enters the brassy regime (Stevenson et al. 2009). Above the bell cutoff the harmonics are typically 20 dB stronger relative to the mouthpiece at ff than at mf (Beauchamp 2012).

**The player** chooses the slide position and the partial, sets the lip resonance near the wanted bore resonance, and sounds about 1.1 per cent above it (Boutin et al. 2015); sets the mouth pressure, which sets the level and, through propagation, the brightness; starts the note with the tongue; and joins notes with the slide, the lips or a light tongue.

## The benchmark

Built first, before any of the instrument, so that every later phase is measured against something fixed. Each target says what is compared, against what, and how close is close enough; where nothing published says, the target is your ear.

**B1. Resonances.** The model's input impedance, computed by driving its mouthpiece with a flow impulse, against the Conn 8H maxima: the first peak near 38 Hz and outside the series; peaks 2 to 7 within 2 per cent of the measured frequencies and within 20 cents of a harmonic series. The model is built on a different instrument's geometry, so this is a plausibility test, not an identity; the exact check is the same geometry computed offline with OpenWInD.

**B2. Where the note sounds.** Played notes sit about 1.1 per cent above the impedance peak (85 per cent of players' samples above it). The lips can bend a note from about 10 to 12 per cent below to about 8 to 9 per cent above its normal frequency (F3 and B flat 3), and the limits fall where the impedance is about 15 per cent of its peak; lipping up past the limit jumps to the next register, lipping down below B flat 2 stops the sound (Boutin et al. 2015, 2020).

**B3. The pedal.** B flat 1 can be played and settles on its own frequency with no resonance under it; the 38 Hz resonance is hard to play (Boutin, Smith and Wolfe 2020; Velut et al. 2017).

**B4. Pressures.** Normal playing of F3 and B flat 3 at a mouth pressure of about 1.3 to 1.4 kPa; the alternating pressure in the mouthpiece about 20 times the mouth's at a low note; mouthpiece levels above 165 dB at fortissimo (Boutin et al. 2015, 2020; Elliott et al. 1982). Each note has a threshold pressure; the form of Fletcher and Tarnopolsky's trumpet law — threshold rising with frequency, then about 15 dB per doubling of pressure, then saturation — is expected to hold, with its constant found from the model.

**B5. Brassiness.** The transfer from mouthpiece to bell, measured on the model at pp, mf and ff: below about 1000 Hz independent of dynamic, above it about 20 dB higher at ff than at mf (Beauchamp 2012); the brassy change between forte and fortissimo (Norman et al. 2010).

**B6. The sound against the Iowa recordings.** For each note and dynamic recorded: the spectral envelope (its rise below the cutoff, the slope above it and how that slope flattens with level), the spectral centroid, the onset time to within 3 dB of the steady level, and the decay at release. Measured from the recordings by a bench written for it, into a table kept in `research/brass/`; the recordings themselves are downloaded, not kept in the repository.

**B7. Joins.** A lip slur takes 80 to 100 ms between steady states, as measured on the horn (Logie et al. 2009); nothing is published for the trombone, so this is provisional.

**B8. Cost.** Real time with margin in a patch of several instruments: no more per voice than a bowed string at the same Fidelity level, measured as the strings are, by the share of real time and the worst block of 256 samples in a real-time run.

**B9. Your ear.** The model's notes set beside the Iowa notes at the same pitch and dynamic, and beside the published demos of the commercial instruments, at a fixed level, one file at a time.

## Phases

### 1. The benchmark — built

`research/brass/trombone-benchmark.md` holds every target with its source, its numbers and what passes. `make brassref` (test/brassref.cpp) reads the twelve Iowa scales from build/iowa-trombone, finds all 99 notes — the threshold between note and silence is the middle of those that find exactly the scale's number of notes, since some scales are digitally silent between notes and some have the room's noise 20 to 30 dB under them — checks each against the note it should be, and writes `research/brass/iowa-tenor-trombone.tsv`. The levels step 13 dB from pp to mf and 8 from mf to ff; the spectral centroid rises 1.55 and 1.85 times; above 1 kHz the spectrum falls about 25 dB an octave at pp and mf and 11 at ff.

### 2. The air column, two ways — built

`make brassbore` (test/brassbore.cpp) measures everything below.

**The calculation in frequency** (`BrassBore`): each section a lossy two-port, multiplied from the bell back to the cup, the radiation at the mouth Silva et al.'s (2009) fit to Levine and Schwinger's unflanged pipe. The wall's losses are the leading boundary-layer terms — the air heavier by 2 sqrt(nu) / (r sqrt(j omega)) and more compressible by 2 (gamma - 1) sqrt(nu / Pr) / (r sqrt(j omega)) — which are the Webster-Lokshin equation's in time, so the timed columns and this are the same physics. It is the reference both timed columns are measured against.

**The geometry.** Smyth and Scott's table alone plays 3 to 6 per cent sharp of the Conn 8H with its upper resonances spread too wide: it has no leadpipe and its mouthpiece was lumped with no length. With a tenor's mouthpiece as tubes — a cup of 5 cubic centimetres, a throat 6.7 mm across and 7 mm long, a backbore widening to 11 mm over 70 mm — and a leadpipe widening to the slide's bore over its first 0.23 m (`BrassBore::tromboneWhole`, typical dimensions, not measured), and the tuning slide pulled out 33 mm, the instrument is 2.746 m from the cup to the bell's mouth, against the Conn's measured 2.74 m, and its first seven resonances are within 25 cents of the Conn's: -11, 0, +6, 0, +8, +23 and +25 cents (B1). For playing in time the cup and the 7 mm throat are lumped, a volume and an inertance, and the backbore stays a tube (`BrassBore::tromboneTimed`): lumped with the throat, its 70 mm went missing and every resonance came out 25 to 65 cents sharp; kept, the timed geometry is within 4 cents of the whole.

**On a grid** (`BrassGrid`): the Webster-Lokshin equation, pressure at points and velocity half way between, the spacing a little over c times the step (a Courant number of 0.999: at exactly 1 it was unstable at 88.2 kHz for one pull of the tuning slide and not another), the half-order derivatives from memory variables at every point — four, fitted by least squares to s to the half between 20 Hz and 0.45 of the rate. The cup, the throat and the first point's half cell are solved together each step: stepped one after another, the throat's inertance against that cell, about 0.14 cubic centimetres, ran away within a few hundred steps. The radiation is an inertance, 0.6133 of the mouth's radius, in parallel with the characteristic resistance, and the calculation it is checked against uses the same. Against the calculation, peaks 2 to 10 within 1.8 cents at 44.1 kHz and 1.4 at 48 and 88.2, the first within 4 cents, the heights within 1 to 4 per cent. Three memory variables give 4.5 cents, two 22. It costs 12 per cent of one core at 44.1 kHz, 14 at 48 and 48 at 88.2, in plain double precision with no vector instructions.

**As delay lines with grids at the ends** (`BrassWaveguide`): two thirds of the trombone — the inner and outer slide tubes, the crook, the gooseneck and the tuning slide's pull — are tubes of one radius each, carried as pairs of delay lines read by third-order Lagrange interpolation; only the backbore and leadpipe (a grid of 38 points at 44.1 kHz) and the tuning slide's cone and the bell (98 points) are grids, which meet the delay lines at ports. The slide's tubes keep their own radii, 6.9, 7.2 and 7.4 mm, and scatter where they meet: made one radius, the resonances moved by up to 30 cents. Each outer tube keeps 2 cm of the inner tube beside it, so that it is never too short to read while the slide is closed; the crook made 7.2 mm instead, to the same end, cost up to 6 cents. The wall's losses over the run of tubes are applied once each way as the wave leaves it, as first-order shelves — each of the half-order fit's high-passes turned into the exponential of itself by a Padé approximant — redesigned when the slide moves. Against the calculation, with the slide closed, 25 cm out and 53 cm out, peaks 2 to 10 within 3 cents, the first within 2 to 8, the heights within 7 per cent. It costs 4.8 per cent of one core at 44.1 kHz and 5.7 at 48, a fifth of the grid's points and two fifths of its computing; and moving the slide is changing two delays.

**The positions**, from the calculation: the slide out 8.3, 17.0, 26.2, 35.8, 45.8 and 56.4 cm for second to seventh, each the extension that lowers the mean of resonances 2 to 8 by a semitone more. Fourth at 26.2 cm lies between Boutin et al.'s measured 22.7 and the estimate of 27.7 from a uniform length.

Not yet measured: the slide moving while the instrument sounds, which needs the lips (phase 3).

### 3. The lips — built

`make brasslips` (test/brasslips.cpp) plays the lips on the delay-line column at 48 kHz and measures them against B2 to B4; `make brasslips ARGS=sweep` sweeps their shape; `ARGS=listen` renders held notes, a slur and a moving slide. Each step the column says what the cup's pressure will be after it as a straight line in the flow (`BrassWaveguide::begin`), the lips solve their flow with it, and the column finishes the step (`end`), so the lips feel the cup within the step.

**One mass blown open** (`BrassLips`): a mass on a spring driven by the pressure across the lips, Bernoulli's flow through the opening and the flow the lip sweeps, Adachi and Sato's closure, Harrison-Harsley's values as Willemsen et al. used them. It plays, but as the one-mass model is known to (Fréour et al. 2023): its notes started at 3.9 to 7.8 kPa, sounded 4 to 9 per cent over their resonances, and F3 could not be bent below its resonance at all; of 48 combinations of quality, mass, breadth and opening, two started a note below 5 kPa, 7.6 and 8.6 per cent sharp. It is kept for comparison.

**A hinged block that swings and stretches** (`BrassHingedLips`): Adachi and Sato's (1996) two-dimensional lip, read from their ATR preprint — a parallelogram jointed at the rim, its tip moving along the flow and across it, driven by the pressure across its length and by the pressure in the opening on its inner face, which falls as the air rushes through and draws the lips together. The swing blows the lips open as one mass does; the stretch lets a note sound below the resonance, as players' lips do (Boutin et al. 2015). Their flow: the jet's energy lost in the cup, a little pressure recovered where it widens, and the air's inertia in the opening. Their Table I's trumpet values, the thickness and joint scaled by the rims' ratio of about one and a half.

**The lips shut from the corners.** The opening's area is 2 b y times w, w = tanh(y / y_c) the open share of the breadth: it grows as the square of the height while the lips are nearly shut and in proportion to it once they are apart, as measured lips do (Fletcher and Tarnopolsky 1999, Bromage et al. 2010) and as Vergez and Rodet (2000) modelled theirs. The damping rises with the shut share from the open lip's to the shut lip's, and the pressure on the inner face is the opening's on the open share and the mouth's on the shut share, so nothing jumps as the lips meet. With the whole breadth shut and the damping changed at a stroke, as Adachi and Sato have it, the flow had a corner and the force a jump at every closure: between the harmonics of a held note at 48 kHz there was power 24 to 33 dB under the harmonics below 2 kHz and 11 to 25 dB under them from 2 to 5 kHz, falling only 4 to 10 dB with each doubling of the rate; now it is 44 to 65 and 35 to 56 dB under (`make brasslips ARGS=aliasing`), most of the gain from the damping. The corners' height is 0.1 mm, the mass 0.9 and the breadth 14 mm, chosen from sweeps against B2 and B4 (`ARGS=sweep`, `ARGS=corners`): higher corners raised the thresholds more than they lowered the aliasing, and lighter lips, which started lower, opened to 400 square millimetres and more at 5.4 kPa, where F3 left its resonance.

Against the benchmark: thresholds rise with the note — 660 Pa on B flat 2, 940 on F3, 1140 on B flat 3, 2120 on the eighth resonance — so mf on F3 and B flat 3 falls near the trombonists' 1.3 kPa (B4); with the lips at 0.98 of the resonance F3 and B flat 3 sound 2.6 and 1.8 per cent over it, and the player sets the lips for the 1.1 per cent players play at (B2); F3 bends from 18.1 per cent under to 11.4 over, B flat 3 from 14.7 under to 9.5 over, against 12.1 and 8.1, 10.0 and 8.9 (B2, wider down than players); the pedal sounds at 56.6 Hz with the lips at 55, 49 cents under B flat 1, with no resonance under it (B3), though with the lips at 60 it goes to a higher note; held notes reach 85 to 111 dB a metre away. At the lowest threshold several resonances start below themselves, in the stretching mode; the player chooses the lips for the note it wants. The slide moved out to fourth position and back under a held B flat 3 glides without a click.

**The lips tuned as a player tunes them.** With the lips held at one setting the note rises in pitch with the pressure, 3 to 5 per cent from 800 Pa to 4.5 kPa. Lowered as the pressure rises, so that the note stays 1.1 per cent over its resonance as players play it — from about 0.98 of the resonance near the threshold to 0.84 at 6.4 kPa on F3 — the notes stay regular to 6.4 kPa (`ARGS=regular` maps where they do). The brassiness measurements below use the lips so tuned, as the player will (phase 6).

Not yet: the tongue, and the tables of lips for each note, which are the player's (phase 6).

### 4. Brassiness — built

`make brasslips ARGS=brass` measures it against B5 and B6, with the steepening and without; `ARGS=aliasing` measures the power between the harmonics, at 48 kHz or a rate given after it; `ARGS=crescendo` renders a crescendo on B flat 3 with and without the steepening, and one loud note at 48 and at 192 kHz.

**The steepening** (`BrassSteepeningDelay`): in the delay lines carrying the wave to the bell through the slide and the gooseneck, each sample arrives after the tube's delay divided by one plus beta p over rho c squared, beta being (gamma + 1) / 2, about 1.2, and p the wave's pressure less its slow mean (under 10 Hz, the steady flow's part). A sample that would overtake the ones ahead of it joins them, and they travel on at the mean of their speeds, arriving at the mean of the times each would have arrived alone: sticky particles, the entropy solution of Burgers' equation, a shock moving at the mean of the speeds either side of it. What comes out at each step is the mean of the curve through the arrivals over that step (Catmull and Rom's cubic on their uneven times), so a front arriving between two steps is shared between them by where it falls; it costs 1.5 dB at 15 kHz.

**The loop stays linear.** Through the slide the wave to the bell is carried twice: as it would travel at no amplitude, which the bell reflects back to the lips, and steepened, which is what the listener hears (phase 5); at each junction the steepened wave meets the same wave coming back as the linear one. So the lips play on a linear column, as Hirschberg et al. (1996) found admissible. With the steepened wave reflected back to the lips instead, loud notes went up to 9 cents flat and, from about 5 kPa on F3, locked into a cycle that repeated to the last digit at a whole number of samples, 273 at 48 kHz and 546 at 96. The column is unchanged at small amplitude: within 3 cents of the calculation. Lips and column, with the listener's filter, cost 7.4 per cent of one core at 48 kHz with the steepening and 7.0 without.

**Against B5 and B6.** mf is the pressure nearest the trombonists' 1.3 kPa (B4); pp and ff are the pressures whose levels are nearest 13 dB under it and 8 dB over it, the recordings' steps. The sound is the listener's, a metre away and 20 degrees off the axis (phase 5). The centroid is over the pitch; the transfer is the listener's harmonics against the mouthpiece's, its change from mf to ff.

### 5. The sound that leaves — built

`make brassradiation` checks the radiation against Levine and Schwinger and shows the listener's filter; `make brasssound` plays every note from E2 to C5 at pp, mf and ff and sets them beside the Iowa recordings (B6); `make brasssound ARGS="listen 20"` writes four files of Iowa's notes and the model's side by side (B9), from notes cut out of the recordings by `make brassref ARGS="cut NOTE DYN OUT.wav"`.

**From the flow at the mouth to the listener** (`BrassRadiation`): a mouth radiating U^2 Re(Z_rad) is heard r away at angle theta from its axis as p^2 = rho c U^2 Re(Z_rad) G(theta) / (4 pi r^2), G the power gain over a source radiating the same power equally every way. Below ka = 3.83 — 1.9 kHz for the bell's 108 mm radius — G and the reflection |R| are Levine and Schwinger's (1948) for an unflanged pipe, computed from their integrals with Bessel's functions; above it the mouth carries more than one mode and the pattern is a flat piston's with Kirchhoff's obliquity, scaled to radiate all the power, the two mixed between ka 3.4 and 3.83. The piston has directions of no sound at each frequency that a bell's curved mouth does not, so the listener hears the mean gain over 10 degrees either side. Checked (`make brassradiation`): Bessel's functions within 3.4e-8 of tabulated values; |R| within 0.005 of Silva et al.'s fit to it; G carrying all the power, to four figures, at every ka from 0.1 to 40; and Levine and Schwinger's own limits on the axis, behind and to the side, to four figures. The small source of phase 4 — the flow's rate of change times rho / (4 pi r) — is right only while ka is small: above about 1 kHz it radiates more power than the bell can, the same in every direction. **One filter from the bell's entrance to the listener** (`BrassListenerFilter`). The bell is fixed and linear, so what reaches the listener from the wave entering it is one filter: the bell's own response, its grid driven at its port with an impulse, from that wave to the flow at its mouth; then the flow's change from step to step, rho / (4 pi r), and the rest of the radiation above, given the least phase its magnitude allows. Truncated where under 60 dB of its energy is left, it is about 500 taps at 48 kHz and costs 0.6 per cent of one core; designing it takes about a second, so each is made once for a bell, rate and listener and shared by every voice. It takes the wave entering the bell — the steepened one — in place of the second bell grid phase 4 had, which cost 4 per cent.

**The listener** is a metre away and 20 degrees off the bell's axis: the angle that brings the model nearest the recordings, whose microphone's place is not given.

**Against the recordings (B6).** Every note from E2 to C5, played where the slide position and resonance put it nearest, the lips tuned to sound it 1.1 per cent over its resonance; mf at 1.3 kPa for a note of 200 Hz, scaled as the frequency to the power three quarters, which Fletcher and Tarnopolsky found keeps a note's spectrum the same from note to note; pp and ff at the recordings' steps of level, 13 dB under mf and 8 over. Measured as the recording bench measures the recordings. The recordings have the room's and the player's noise under every note, and where their harmonics fall under it the bench finds the noise, so the slope over 1 kHz here counts only the harmonics within 40 dB of the strongest, in both. The medians by register and dynamic, the recordings first and the model second:

Over the nine, the model's centroid is 17 per cent from the recordings', its slope 3.3 dB an octave and its share 3.0 dB; on the axis 20 per cent, 3.4 and 3.3; at 45 degrees 24 per cent, 6.4 and 2.8. The middle register at pp and mf is within 12 per cent in centroid and 4.4 dB an octave in slope. Two things are wrong. The low register at ff is too dark: the recordings' steps give it only 1.3 to 1.7 kPa, where its brassiness has barely begun, which is the level against the pressure again (phase 4). The high register has too much over 1 kHz, by 4 to 10 dB, most at pp: its notes are already rich at the pressure that sounds them, where a player's pp there is nearly pure. Both are the player's (phase 6) — what pressure each dynamic takes, and lips set for a soft high note — or the lips'.

**Beside the recordings (B9):** B flat 2, F3, B flat 3 and F4, each the Iowa note at pp then the model's at the same loudness, then the two at mf, then at ff. The model's notes start and stop with the pressure alone, 30 and 50 ms: onsets and releases wait for the tongue (phase 6).

### 6. The player — built

`make brassplay` plays a B flat scale over two octaves tongued and slurred, every dynamic on one note, and glissandi; its modes judge every kind of slur (`slurs`), starts across the range (`starts`), one start or one slur closely (`attack`, `join`), and how loud it gets (`peaks`). `make brassrender ARGS="score.mxl SECONDS"` plays a score's trombone part, read by mpxScore over the cable, with its piano part on mpxPiano.

**The trombone as a player holds it** (`BrassInstrument`): the air column, the lips, and three controls — the slide's extension, the breath in the mouth, and the tongue, a valve between the breath and the lips, nought shut and one clear. The lips feel the breath in proportion to how far the tongue is open. While the slide moves, the column is told every eighth of a millisecond, so a glissando is continuous.

**The tables** (`BrassTable`, `src/TromboneTable.cpp`, made by `make brasstables`): for each of the 98 positions and resonances the slide can sound, the threshold pressure, and on a ladder of pressures a quarter octave apart, the lips that sound the note 1.1 per cent over its resonance, where it sounds, how loud and how bright. Made with the tuning slide pulled 55 mm, which puts F3, B flat 3 and A flat 4 in first position within 7 cents of A440.

**Where each note is played** (`BrassPlayer`). Every resonance that can sound the note is a candidate. On each, the slide is placed where the tables say that resonance sounds the note in tune, on a straight line in the logarithm of the pitch between positions, so the positions are continuous, not seven stops. Short extensions are preferred, then the least movement from the note before, then joins that need no help; the note after is looked at too.

**How hard.** The written dynamic is a brightness: the spectral centroid the recordings have at that dynamic in the note's register, the median over its notes. From E2 to B2 it is 590 Hz at pp, 877 at mf and 1526 at ff; from C3 to B3, 602, 973 and 1927; from C4 to C5, 676, 1039 and 1926; on straight lines between the registers' middles, and carried on beyond pp and ff for ppp and fff. Over every pitch the medians are 628, 982 and 1839 Hz (B6), but the low register's ff is 300 Hz under that. The player blows the pressure at which the tables give that centroid and sets the lips the tables give for it, so a loud note is brassy because it is blown hard.

**How loud.** Blown so, the model's markings are further apart in level than the recordings', most at the top: measured over the steady middle of each note (`make brassplay ARGS="ladder raw"`), as `make brassref` measures the recordings, its ff is 8.6 dB over its mf from E2 to B2, 11.7 from C3 to B3 and 16.0 from C4 to C5, where the recordings' is 8.0, 8.5 and 6.8; its pp is 11.5, 13.5 and 14.8 under, where theirs is 12.6, 13.8 and 11.4; and its fff a further 5 to 11 dB over its ff. So each note's sound is turned up or down by a gain for its marking and register, which brings every marking to the recordings' level against mf, the brightness left as the blowing made it. Over mf the gain is from the note's own level in the tables at its pressure, against at mf's, since one note can need far more breath than its neighbours for the same brightness; the tables are within half a decibel of the played level from mp up, but as much as 8 dB under it at pp high up, so under mf the register's figures are used. The recordings have only pp, mf and ff: the markings between are spaced evenly, and ppp and fff are one more step beyond. The gain glides over 40 ms as each note begins, and is put on the sound alone, after everything rehearsed or judged. With it (`make brassplay ARGS=ladder`), pp is 12.4 dB under mf and ff 7.8 over, against the recordings' 13.1 and 7.8, and ppp to fff is 28 dB, where blown alone it was 39.

**Joins.** Tongued: the tongue shuts, the slide moves and the lips set behind it, and it lets go over 1.5 ms for an accent, 3 ordinarily and 8 soft. Slurred on one position, the lip slur: the lips glide to the next resonance with the breath pushed a little. Slurred with the slide moving: the natural slur, where the slide and the resonance move the pitch opposite ways, or the legato tongue, the tongue half closing while the slide moves. Written as a glissando: the slide moves through the last part of the note before on one resonance. A note ends with the breath falling over 45 ms, sooner for a staccato or before a tongued note close after, so the slide has time to move. In a quick passage — where the slide's move and the tongue's closing would take more than half the time from one note's start to the next — the slide moves as fast as it can, about 5 m/s, and the tongue shuts only 10 ms before it: B flat 2 to C3, first position to sixth, as eighths at 144 a minute, otherwise left the B flat 50 ms, and it never spoke.

**What a start should be like** (`BrassStart`, `src/TromboneStarts.cpp`, made by `make brassref ARGS=starts`). Measured on the Iowa recordings' tongued notes, every register and dynamic, from the moment each comes within 30 dB of the level it settles at, every 5 ms to 200 ms: the level against the settled level; the pitch against the settled pitch, by YIN (de Cheveigné and Kawahara 2002); how surely it repeats, YIN's sureness; and the brightness, the centroid against the settled one. The template for a register and dynamic is the median of the recordings at each moment and half the distance between their quartiles. A trombonist's start rises over about 100 ms; comes in 40 to 80 cents sharp and falls into tune within 30 to 50 ms; repeats surely from about 20 ms; and begins an octave and a half darker than it settles, brightening as it grows.

**How the model starts.** Its lips build far quicker than a player's. With the breath stepped to its mark, the first 15 ms are a puff of air with no pitch, and the pitch then wobbles by up to 100 cents for 60 ms. With the breath rising from under the threshold, the lips sputter quietly until it is well over it and then sound all at once. So the breath starts a little over the threshold, 1.3 or 2 times it, where the lips sound at once but softly, the lips set for that pressure; and both rise to the note's mark, the breath by equal ratios, quicker at first, over 40 to 300 ms, held to 80 ms less than half the note's length but allowed 60 ms for a quick note, under 0.45 s — any quicker and its low notes pop out late and sharp — and held to 25 ms for an accent. An accent's breath is 12 per cent over its mark for a moment, a heavy accent's 25: a quarter over sent F sharp 2 nearly a semitone sharp and 5 dB over the note.

**Rehearsal.** A start or a slur met for the first time is tried on copies of everything as it will be 200 ms before the note: a start sixty ways (the lips set up to 6 per cent over the note, the breath stepped or rising from 0.5, 1.3 or 2 times the threshold over 40 to 300 ms, a little over its mark or not), each played half a second and measured as the recordings were, the one nearest the template played — with 5 hundredths of a spread added for every millisecond it is heard later than 25 ms after the tongue lets go, since its shape is measured from where it is heard and a note whose lips sputter for 80 ms and then sound all at once looked like a good start; a quick note's start is judged instead over its own length by how soon and how cleanly it speaks, and by how far its front stands over the note, since the recordings' starts are of long notes; a slur about a hundred ways (its own kind's and the legato tongue's), each played 250 ms and judged by `BrassJudge` — how long until its pitch holds within 20 cents for 40 ms, how long it is between the two pitches, rough or off both, and how long it falls 20 dB under the quieter note. The trials are shared over a crew of threads. The best way is remembered for its situation — the join, the resonance, the slide to 2 cm, the dynamic's step, and the resonance and place of the note before — and played again without trying. Over 18 starts from B flat 2 to B flat 4 at pp, mf and ff, the mean distance from the templates is 2.01 of their spreads, against 3.59 with every note's breath stepped; over 12 kinds of slur, the new pitch holds within 10 ms on average, with 15 ms between the pitches and none falling 20 dB.

**Still unlike the recordings.** At ff the level still reaches its mark within 10 to 20 ms of being heard where the recordings take about 100, and the brightness with it.

**Vibrato** by the slide: 10 cents slight and 22 wide, at 5.5 Hz, from 180 ms into a note long enough.

### 7. The module — built

**mpxTrombone** (`src/mpxTrombone.cpp`), laid out as the bowed strings are: eight HP, one column, every name in capitals in the heading face — MPX IN, then GATE, V/OCT and LEVEL for playing without a cable, the brightness knob BRT, the level lamp and OUT — with Fidelity, Economy and the diagnostics in the right-click menu. A chord on the cable is played as its top note; a note below E2 or above F sharp 5 an octave or more in.

**On a thread of its own** (`BrassEngine`). A rehearsal takes about a second of computing, so at Fidelity levels 1 and 2 the whole player runs on a thread of its own, rendering ahead of what is heard into a ring that the audio thread only reads from. While notes are announced it renders 3.5 s ahead, a second short of how far ahead the cable announces, and a rehearsal comes out of that store. It times its rehearsals, and with less in hand than half a second more than one and a half times the longest of them lately, a note met for the first time is played untried, from memory or by habit, and counted on the panel: a rehearsal that ran past what was in hand would be heard as silence. After five seconds with nothing announced it renders only 30 ms ahead, more than Rack asks for at once, so a note from a keyboard sounds 30 ms late. A withdrawal — the score stopped or taken back — fades the output at once; the player ends its note and forgets the rest, and what it had rendered ahead is skipped. Level 1 tries every way; level 2, the default, a third of them; level 3 has no thread, the player runs on the audio thread, and nothing is tried or needed ahead. Through the first minute of Saint-Saëns's Cavatine, driven in time with the clock as Rack drives it (`make brassrender ARGS="score.mxl 60 engine=2"`), level 2 played nothing as silence and tried every note it had not met before; level 1 played nothing as silence but 74 of its 137 notes untried, for want of time, on a machine of ten cores. Economy leaves out the steepening, for less computing and less brassiness at the loudest.

**Tuning by ear.** Each note's tongued start, tone and lips can be set by ear and kept, pitch by pitch and marking by marking. The start: RISE, how long the breath takes to reach its level from just over the threshold, up to the note's length less 80 ms, the lips at the note's setting; BREATH, the pressure against what the marking asks, from −10 to +4 dB. The lips: TENSION, the lips' own frequency as a share of the resonance's, from 0.80 to 1.10, the player's muscle tone; GAP, how far apart they are at rest, from 0.2 mm pressed together to 0.5 apart, nought just touching as the trombone's own; DAMP, one over their quality open; and MASS, one for the whole trombone, as a player's lips weigh what they weigh, kept with the settings and not changed from note to note. Tension and gap change how the lips move in each cycle, and so the tone, and pull the pitch, as the breath does, which the slide puts right as a player's does. When the note, BREATH or the lips' knobs change, once the knobs have been still half a second, `BrassPlayer::tuneFor` finds, in two to four seconds, the breath at which the note reaches its marking's brightness with those lips, and where the slide sounds it in tune at their tension and the breath BREATH sets: at six pressures from 0.8 to 4 times the tables', the slide halved between 150 cents in and 150 out, then put right twice by the pitch heard after 0.8 s, since heavy lips are still settling at 0.4 s, and up to three times more at the breath as BREATH trims it. A tension at which the lips sound the note at none of the pressures is played all the same, to be heard so, and the display says WILL NOT SOUND; one the slide cannot follow is left out of tune, and the display says by how much. The panel's tuning knobs are in two rows: RISE, BREATH and MASS; TENSION, GAP and DAMP. The yellow BUSY lamp, between TENSION and GAP, is lit from the moment one of them other than RISE is turned until the note has been tuned for it. With TUNE lit, every note from the cable is played the moment it arrives, live, from the knobs, so a turn is heard on the next note; when the music reaches a pitch and marking the knobs go to what is kept for it, or else stay as they are while the note is tuned for their lips and then go to the best of the clean starts a search finds for it with those lips in about five seconds (`BrassPlayer::startsFor`: the breath rising over 40 to 300 ms, each played from silence and measured against the recordings' starts; the module offers those with the lips at the note's setting), which TRY steps through; SAVE keeps the knobs' settings for the pitch and marking playing, with the breath and the slide found for them and the mass they were found for, in the Rack user folder under DreamerMPX/trombone-tuning.json, where the trombone's mass is kept too; and while REF is held each note that starts is the Iowa recording of its pitch at its marking (cut into DreamerMPX/trombone-reference by `make brassref ARGS="cutall DIR"`), as loud as the model has played that note. A kept note found for another mass is tuned again when it next plays while tuning. With TUNE unlit, the settings kept are played wherever a note has them, untried, with the mass they were found for; a note with none is played by the tables, with the trombone's own lips. The tuning scores, two bars of quarter notes on every pitch from E2 to C5 at 80 a minute, each pitch a section named after it, are made by `tools/trombone-tuning-score.py` into research/brass/tuning. This is the start table the open questions ask about, filled by ear.

**The level.** A loud trombone's peaks stand far over its level — the shock fronts of brassiness: at ff 5 to 13 Pa where its root mean square is 1.3 to 1.9, at fff 9 to 20 (`make brassplay ARGS=peaks`). The output is half a volt a pascal, which puts the loudest fff peaks at 10 V, the highest of them rounded by the soft clip.

### 8. Afterwards

The bass trombone (the F and D valves as switched tubes joined at a valve junction, Bilbao 2011), mutes (straight, cup, Harmon, plunger: a changed bell reflection with a cavity resonance, and a filter on what leaves — Campbell, Gilbert and Myers 2021), then the trumpet (designed in docs/trumpet.md), the flugelhorn and the horn on the same engine.

**Articulations**, in this order: the slurs and the glissando, rebuilt to join two notes of the settings table; the accent, heavy accent, staccato, tenuto and ghost notes; the scoop into a note from below or above, the fall off its end down and the doit up, and the drawn bend, which the slide follows exactly or the lips a little; grace notes and tremolo, fast repeated tonguing. Then markings the cable does not yet carry: the lip trill and the shake, between neighbouring resonances and only high up; flutter tonguing and the growl; the breath attack without the tongue; fp and sforzando; the swell within a held note; the rip, a fast lip glide up through the resonances into a note; and the pedal notes, which are now played an octave up.

**Markings made for other instruments** are translated where brass has an equivalent, so that a part written for one, a guitar's from Guitar Pro say, plays sensibly: a hammer-on or pull-off is a slur; a legato slide a glissando; a shift slide a glissando re-tongued as it arrives; a dead note a ghost note; let ring tenuto; pizzicato staccato. Palm muting, harmonics, tapping, strums and rolled chords have none and are left out, the note played plainly.

## Tests

As for the bowed strings, each phase leaves a bench that measures what it claims: the reference table from the recordings (phase 1); the impedance of each air column against B1 and its cost (2); held notes at fixed pressure and lip frequency against B2 to B4 (3); the transfer at pp, mf and ff against B5 (4); notes against the recordings (5); phrases with the unrehearsed count and REC as the strings have them (6 and 7).

## Open questions

- Whether one lip mass is enough over the trombone's range, or the second degree of freedom the measurements describe is needed.
- How far the two published lip parameter sets, a factor of 2.5 apart, can be reconciled by the benchmark.
- Whether nonlinear propagation applied to the outgoing wave alone is enough, or the reflected wave's steepening matters for the lips at fortissimo.
- The low register at ff too dark and the high register too bright, most at pp, against the recordings (phase 5): the pressures the player gives each dynamic, or lips set differently for soft high notes.
- Where the Iowa microphone stood: with the lips as they now are, 10 degrees off the axis fits the recordings' harmonics best, on the axis F4's top is 11 dB too bright and at 20 degrees B flat 3's is 5.5 dB short.
- The level does not level off at high pressure as the trumpet's does, and the lips open to 330 square millimetres at 5.4 kPa and stay open most of each cycle; whether they should shut for longer at ff.
- The slide's position for each note: Boutin et al. give 22.7 cm for fourth position, a uniform-length estimate 27.7 cm.
- The bell's length in Smyth and Scott, 50.2 cm in one table and 56.7 in the other.
- Whether a moving slide's Doppler shift and volume change are audible; nothing has been measured.
- Whether tables computed with GPL tools may be shipped in an MIT plugin.
- Trombone blowing pressures from pp to ff, brass vibrato and tonguing rates: not published, to be found from the model, from recordings, or by ear.
- Whether the starts need rehearsing at all. A player does not try a note before playing it, and does not know where the waves in the instrument are; from the note, how loud it is and what comes before it, they choose how fast the breath rises and how the lips are set and move. As the bowed strings' starts can come from a table, the trombone's might: the start a rehearsal finds best for each note, dynamic and kind of join, found once and looked up while playing. Rehearsal would then be for checking the table, not for playing.
