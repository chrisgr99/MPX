# Brass instruments: physics and modelling methods common to all brass

Compiled 13 October 2026 as survey 1 of 5 for the planned MPX brass models (trumpet, flugelhorn, trombone, French horn). This survey covers the physics shared by all lip-driven instruments and the numerical methods used to simulate them. Instrument-specific material (surveys 2 to 4) and the player model, mutes and existing real-time products (survey 5) are covered elsewhere.

Conventions. A statement followed by an author and year is stated by that source. A statement marked "inference" is my own reasoning or arithmetic from the cited numbers. A statement marked "unverified" comes from memory or from a secondary mention and was not checked against the primary source. The reference list at the end gives the reading status of every source: read in part (the relevant sections of the full text), abstract only, or known only through another paper's reference list or a catalogue record. The web search budget for this session ran out before every citation could be checked, so some bibliographic details remain marked as unverified.

## Summary

A brass instrument is a self-sustained oscillator made of three parts: the player's lips, which act as a pressure-controlled valve; the air column (mouthpiece, leadpipe, cylindrical and conical tubing, bell), which is a nearly linear resonator described by its input impedance; and the bell, which sets which frequencies are reflected back to the lips and which are radiated. The same source-resonator split, with a nonlinear excitation coupled to a linear delay-based resonator, is the one MPX already uses for bowed strings (McIntyre, Schumacher and Woodhouse 1983).

The lips are most often modelled as a single mass on a spring with damping, pushed open by mouth pressure ("outward-striking"), with Bernoulli flow through the opening. Measurements on players and artificial lips show more than one lip resonance and a mixture of outward-striking and sideways or inward-striking behaviour, so two-degree-of-freedom models (Adachi and Sato 1996) describe the high register better. Players sound slightly above the bore resonance they select, about 1.1 percent above the impedance peak on average for trombone (Boutin et al. 2015).

The bore's resonances form a nearly complete harmonic series except for the lowest one, which lies well below the series. The pedal note is played at half the frequency of the second resonance and is not supported by a resonance of its own. The mouthpiece adds a broad resonance that raises the impedance in the playing range, and the bell sets a cutoff above which little is reflected.

At loud dynamics, nonlinear propagation in the narrow cylindrical tubing steepens the outgoing wave into a shock, which produces the "brassy" timbre (Hirschberg et al. 1996). How much an instrument does this depends on its bore profile and can be summarised by the brassiness potential parameter of Gilbert, Myers, Campbell and Pyle. Several efficient ways to simulate this exist; the cheapest apply an amplitude-dependent time warp to the outgoing wave in the cylindrical section.

For the plugin, the most likely candidate (inference, argued in the last sections) is a digital waveguide bore built from measured bore profiles, with lumped wall-loss filters, a bell reflection and transmission filter pair fitted offline, a one-mass lip valve with Bernoulli flow (with a two-mass option), and a simple-wave nonlinear section in the cylindrical tubing. This costs a few hundred floating-point operations per sample per voice. Full finite-difference models (Bilbao and Chick 2013; Harrison et al. 2015) are more general and handle partly-pressed valves, but cost one to two orders of magnitude more and were measured at about real time for a trumpet and 3.6 times slower than real time for a 4.5 m horn on a 2014 laptop processor.

## 1. The system in outline

The canonical description of a wind instrument is a nonlinear excitation coupled to a linear resonator through feedback (McIntyre, Schumacher and Woodhouse 1983). For brass, Elliott and Bowsher (1982) gave the model that most later time-domain simulations start from: a lip oscillator driven by the difference between mouth pressure and mouthpiece pressure, a flow through the lip opening, and a bore described by its input impedance or by its time-domain reflection function. Vergez and Rodet (2001) describe their real-time trumpet model as derived from Elliott and Bowsher, with a single lip mass, a spring and a damper, the bore represented by its measured reflection function, and Bernoulli coupling between flow, lip aperture and pressure difference.

Harrison et al. (2015) divide the instrument into a resonator (bore and valves), a generator (lips) and a radiator (the flaring bell), following Fletcher and Rossing (1998). The lips oscillate near one of the resonances of the bore. The bell determines the efficiency of transfer of sound to the room and also changes the frequencies and bandwidths of the resonances.

Textbook-level treatments of all the material below are given by Campbell, Gilbert and Myers (2021), a book entirely on brass instruments that develops the modelling at a mathematical level, and by Chaigne and Kergomard (2016). A review article is Campbell (2004). Moore (2016) gives a short introduction in Acoustics Today that was read for this survey.

## 2. The lip reed

### 2.1 Valve types

Pressure-controlled valves in wind instruments are classified by how the valve responds to an increase in the upstream (mouth) and downstream (mouthpiece) pressures (Fletcher 1979; Fletcher 1993; both known here only through reference lists and summaries, so the wording below is the classification as it is commonly stated). Reeds have been divided into "striking inward" and "striking outward" families since Helmholtz (Chen and Weinreich 1996, abstract). A reed that the mouth pressure pushes closed is called inward-striking (the clarinet reed). A valve that the mouth pressure pushes open is called outward-striking (the "swinging door" model of the lips). A valve that moves perpendicular to the flow and is pulled shut by the Bernoulli pressure in the channel is called sideways- or upward-striking. Fletcher (1993) identified conditions under which each type can sustain small-amplitude oscillation (summary by Coyle 2024, abstract only). The result used throughout the brass literature is that an outward-striking valve sounds above its own mechanical resonance frequency and an inward-striking valve sounds below it; the lip measurements of Newton et al. (section 2.5) are consistent with this.

### 2.2 One-mass models

In the one-mass model the lip is a single mass on a spring with a damper. The driving force is the pressure difference across the lips multiplied by an effective area. Harrison et al. (2015) write it as follows: the lip displacement obeys a damped harmonic oscillator equation with a damping coefficient and a natural lip frequency, and the forcing term is an effective lip surface area times the pressure difference between mouth and mouthpiece, divided by the lip mass. The volume flow into the instrument has two parts: the Bernoulli flow through the opening, equal to the opening height (clipped at zero when the lips close) times the channel width times the square root of twice the absolute pressure difference over air density, signed by the pressure difference; and a "swept" flow equal to the effective lip area times the lip velocity. They attribute the form to Adachi and Sato (1995).

The flow model assumes laminar flow through the lip channel and a jet that dissipates all its kinetic energy by turbulence in the mouthpiece cup, with no pressure recovery (Vergez and Rodet 2001, following Hirschberg). Vergez and Rodet (2001) also make the effective length of the mass depend on the opening, to account for Poiseuille-type behaviour at small openings.

The Synthesis ToolKit Brass class (Cook and Scavone, after Cook's TBone, 1991) is a reduced form of the same idea and is useful as a minimum reference. Read from the source code: the breath pressure (an ADSR envelope plus vibrato) is scaled, the returning bore pressure from a single delay line is scaled by 0.85, their difference is passed through a two-pole resonator tuned to the lip frequency with pole radius 0.997 (standing in for the lip mass-spring system), the result is squared and clipped at 1 (standing in for the position-to-area mapping and the valve's saturation), and that value sets a mix between mouth pressure and bore pressure that is passed through a DC blocker into the delay line. The delay is set to about two periods of the target note plus three samples, so the lip filter has to select a harmonic of a single-tube resonator. This model has no bell, no wall loss beyond the scaling, and no Bernoulli flow; it is cheap and stable but does not reproduce the impedance structure of a real bore (inference from the code).

### 2.3 Two-dimensional and two-mass models

Adachi and Sato (1996) give the lip two degrees of freedom: a swinging motion (rotation about a joint, which opens the lips outward with the flow) and a stretching motion (perpendicular to the flow). Read from their preprint: the lip is a parallelogram whose mass sits at its centre, with one stiffness and one quality factor; when the lips touch, an extra restoring force with three times the stiffness acts, and the quality factor drops below 1. The model lets the lips oscillate on either side of a bore resonance. In their simulations of a trumpet, sounding frequencies fall on both sides of the resonances in the first three modes, and only on the lower side from the fourth mode upward; the lower modes behave like an outward-striking valve and the higher modes like a laterally striking valve, consistent with Yoshikawa's measurements (Adachi and Sato 1996). An earlier paper by the same authors gives the time-domain method (Adachi and Sato 1995).

Two-mass models of the kind used for vocal folds have been tried for brass lips. Vilain et al. (2003) determined two-mass model parameters from dynamic measurements and reported that the two-mass model did not reproduce the measured dynamics of a lip replica, which they attribute to the mechanical boundary conditions (abstract only). Boutin et al. (2015) found that the lip motion of ten trombonists is consistent with two-degree-of-freedom models whose effective lengths vary, and that this explains why lips can self-oscillate against an inertive load, a compliant load, or no downstream resonator at all (abstract and parts of the paper read).

Lopes and Hélie (2016) derive the air jet between moving lips from the Euler equations with boundary conditions on a moving wall, reduce it to a finite-dimensional system that preserves the power balance, and couple it to a mass-spring-damper lip and a pipe in port-Hamiltonian form (abstract only). This is a more detailed flow model than Bernoulli, intended for energy-consistent simulation; see section 7.6.

### 2.4 Flow details that matter for a model

Bromage, Campbell and Gilbert (2010) measured the open area between vibrating lips of trombone players and of an artificial mouth with high-speed video through a transparent mouthpiece, to fix the exponent in a power law relating open area to the lip separation; one-degree-of-freedom simulations usually assume a fixed relation (abstract only). Stevenson et al. (2009, title known from the HAL record, venue unverified) report that the open-area behaviour does not change much when the instrument enters the brassy regime (secondary summary).

Boutin et al. (2015) report three findings that bear directly on a model. The inertance of the air in the lip channel is usually negligible, so the flow can be treated as quasi-steady. The flow swept by the moving lips contributes a significant part of the total flow at low pitches, so the swept-flow term in 2.2 should be kept. Players normally sounded about 1.1 percent above the frequency of the bore impedance peak, and could bend the pitch above or below it without a discontinuity.

The upstream airway (vocal tract) also couples to the lips. Fréour and Scavone (2013) measured the coupling between the lips, the downstream air column and the upstream airways in trombone playing; Fréour's thesis reports that a vocal-tract resonance can move the playing frequency close to a lip mechanical resonance (thesis abstract). An artificial player with active control of the upstream impedance was used by Fréour, Lopes, Hélie, Caussé and Scavone (2015, abstract only).

### 2.5 Measured lip properties

Yoshikawa (1995) measured the phase between mouthpiece pressure and lip displacement to decide between outward and upward striking. Lip-driven pipes without a mouthpiece support both types because the lips are very flexible; mouthpiece-and-tube systems supported only outward striking; the French horn and trumpet showed outward striking in the lowest mode and upward striking in higher modes, and the upward-striking type became dominant as blowing pressure increased (abstract). Copley and Strong (1996) made a stroboscopic study of trombone lip vibration (reference-list entry; JASA 99, 1219 to 1226). Chen and Weinreich (1996) used a Helmholtz resonator with active feedback as a one-mode brass instrument and measured the playing frequencies of three trumpet players (abstract seen on a third-party page; volume and pages unverified).

Gilbert, Ponthus and Petiot (1998) built an artificial trombone player, measured the mechanical response of the artificial lips for different embouchures, and compared the lip resonances with the frequencies played, with a mouthpiece alone and with a trombone (abstract). Cullen, Gilbert and Campbell (2000) measured the mechanical response of artificial lips and found several resonance peaks, each of which could be classed as inward- or outward-striking; as the trombone slide was moved, the threshold frequency moved smoothly between the two types, which the authors say a single-degree-of-freedom lip is unlikely to explain (abstract).

Newton, Campbell and Gilbert measured the mechanical response of the lips of four semi-professional trombonists and of artificial lips by high-speed video. Every measurement showed a strong outward-striking resonance, and the played note was always above it; several also showed a weaker inward-striking resonance above the played note. Human lip resonances had lower quality factors than artificial ones (HAL abstract of the 2007 manuscript; published version given from memory as JASA 123, EL14 to EL20, 2008, unverified; same work in Newton's 2009 Edinburgh thesis). Richards (2003) is an Edinburgh thesis on computational lip models and artificial-mouth experiments (record only).

### 2.6 How the lip resonance relates to the played note

Combining the sources above: in normal playing the lowest strong lip resonance lies below the sounding frequency (outward-striking behaviour), the bore impedance peak lies a little below the sounding frequency, and the player can move the sounding frequency to either side of the peak by changing the lip tension (Newton et al.; Boutin et al. 2015). In the high register the behaviour shifts toward sideways or upward striking (Yoshikawa 1995; Adachi and Sato 1996).

Adachi and Sato's trumpet simulations give concrete numbers (read from Table III of the preprint). For the second mode the input impedance peak is at 232 Hz, the lip eigenfrequency that gave the most stable oscillation was 250 Hz, and the sounding frequency was 236 Hz; for the third mode 341, 370 and 342 Hz; fourth 457, 490, 453 Hz; fifth 572, 580, 563 Hz; sixth 686, 680, 673 Hz. A "loose-lipping" tone at 166 Hz was produced with the lip set to 160 Hz. In their model the lip eigenfrequency was swept from 60 to 700 Hz and blowing pressures for a moderate level were 2.0 to 5.0 kPa for modes 1 to 6. By my arithmetic, in all five optimum oscillations the model's lip eigenfrequency lies 1 to 8 percent above the sounding frequency, and only the loose-lipping tone has it below. This is the opposite order from the strong outward-striking resonance measured on real lips, which lies below the played note (Newton et al.). Inference: the single eigenfrequency of the two-dimensional model is not directly comparable with a measured lip resonance, because the swinging motion supplies the outward-striking phase behaviour even when the eigenfrequency is above the note (Adachi and Sato report positive phase differences in the lower modes); lip frequencies taken from this model should not be read as measured values.

Linear stability analysis (LSA) of the lip and bore system predicts the threshold blowing pressure and the frequency at which oscillation starts. Cullen et al. (2000) used it with one lip mode and one bore mode. Velut, Vergez, Gilbert and Djahanbani (2017) used an outward-striking valve coupled to a modal air column and found good agreement between LSA and nonlinear time-domain simulation near threshold; well above threshold the agreement was looser, with overblowing, quasi-periodicity and period doubling. Their LSA predicted the production of the trombone pedal note, for which only nonlinear explanations had been proposed before (abstract). Inference: LSA on a fitted modal model of the bore is a cheap way for a player model to choose, before a note, the lip frequency and the minimum pressure needed for each target note, which is the same role the rehearsal step plays in the MPX bowed strings.

## 3. The air column

### 3.1 Input impedance

The input impedance is the ratio of acoustic pressure to volume flow at the mouthpiece entry, as a function of frequency. Its peaks are the resonances available to the lips; their heights and widths set how strongly each resonance controls the lips. The time-domain equivalent is the reflection function (the pressure returned to the mouthpiece in response to an incident pressure pulse), from which the input impedance follows by Fourier transform. Adachi and Sato (1996) and Vergez and Rodet (2001) both drive their lip models with a reflection function computed from a measured input impedance. Adachi and Sato note that a reflection function computed this way leaks into negative time because of the finite bandwidth and the abrupt reflections from the mouthpiece, and they replace it with a causal version (preprint, section II).

### 3.2 Cylindrical and conical sections

In a cylinder, plane waves travel without reflection, so a cylinder is exactly a pair of delay lines plus losses. In a cone, spherical waves travel without reflection, so a cone is also a pair of delays, but junctions between cones of different taper produce frequency-dependent reflections; Gilbert, Kergomard and Polack (1990) give the reflection functions at discontinuities in conical bores. Only the cone and the cylinder support true travelling waves; a flaring bell does not (Smith, Physical Audio Signal Processing, Bell Models page, citing Putland). Waveguide models therefore approximate a bore as a chain of cylinders and cones (Berners 1999; van Walstijn and Smith 1998; van Walstijn and Campbell 2003) or use a flared-pipe element derived from the Webster-Lokshin equation (Hélie, Mignot and Matignon 2007; Mignot, Hélie and Matignon 2010).

### 3.3 The mouthpiece

A brass mouthpiece has a cup, a narrow throat and a backbore that widens into the leadpipe (Moore 2016). The cup volume acts as an acoustic compliance and the throat as an inertance; together with flow resistance they form a resonance in the same way as an inductor, capacitor and resistor (Moore 2016). In Moore's simulation of a trumpet-like tube, adding the mouthpiece substantially raises the input impedance between 200 Hz and 1.5 kHz, the normal playing range of the trumpet; a shallower cup raises the mouthpiece resonance and makes high notes easier (Moore 2016). The leadpipe in a trumpet is about 25 cm long and continues the expansion of the bore, but most of the expansion happens in the mouthpiece (Moore 2016).

The mouthpiece behaves approximately as a Helmholtz resonator: a larger cup lowers its resonance ("pop tone") and so does a narrower throat; once fitted to the instrument it lowers the resonances that lie above the pop tone and strengthens those near it (UNSW brass acoustics page, Wolfe). Secondary teaching sources found during the search give trumpet mouthpiece pop frequencies of roughly 750 to 850 Hz, about 800 Hz for a Bach 1 1/4 C (unverified, not from a primary source). The lip protruding into the cup changes the effective cup volume; the UNSW page says there is no formal study of this effect.

For a model, the mouthpiece can be represented either as a lumped compliance and inertance at the bore entry or as a few short conical and cylindrical sections from a measured profile. Smyth and Scott (2011) leave the mouthpiece out of their measured trombone model because its effective geometry changes with the vibrating lips and so cannot be measured as a fixed element.

### 3.4 Bore profile, mode tuning and the pedal note

A cylinder closed at one end has resonances at odd multiples of its lowest frequency. The flaring bell shortens the effective length more at low frequencies than at high ones, because long wavelengths are reflected earlier in the flare; this raises the low resonances most and compresses the series, and the mouthpiece lowers the upper resonances. A well-designed instrument ends with resonances 2 to 8 and above in nearly the ratios 2, 3, 4, 5 and so on, while the lowest resonance stays well below the series (Moore 2016; UNSW page). Moore calls this "a harmonic series with a detuned fundamental".

Adachi and Sato's measured trumpet impedance shows this numerically (Table II of the preprint): impedance peaks at 87, 232, 341, 457, 572, 686, 800 and 913 Hz. By my arithmetic the spacing of peaks 2 to 8 is close to 114 Hz, giving ratios of about 2.04, 2.99, 4.01, 5.02, 6.02, 7.02 and 8.01, while the first peak sits at about 0.76 of the spacing. Bilbao and Chick (2013, Table I) list measured impedance maxima for a B-flat trumpet at 83.5, 234.2, 353.1, 469.1, 591.6, 702.7 and 812.5 Hz, for a tenor trombone in first position at 38.3, 113.2, 174.0, 233.2, 296.0, 350.9 and 410.1 Hz, and for a baroque horn crooked in D at 23.2, 64.8, 103.7, 144.7, 181.9, 220.3 and 256.2 Hz.

The pedal note is not supported by any resonance. On the bass trombone the first impedance peak is far too flat to play and the pedal note, near B-flat 1, is half the frequency of the second peak near 116 Hz; the note is sustained by the higher resonances, whose spacing equals the pedal frequency, and its spectrum has almost no power at the fundamental (UNSW page). Velut et al. (2017) found that linear stability analysis also predicts a trombone pedal note.

The resonances weaken with frequency because the bell transmits more and reflects less at high frequency; on a trombone the impedance peaks disappear above about 700 to 800 Hz (UNSW page).

### 3.5 Bends and valves

Félix, Dalmont and Nederveen (2012) show that the effect of a bend on the resonances is frequency dependent and mostly, but not always, small (as summarised by Bilbao and Chick 2013, who ignored bends for the trombone and horn and noted that the assumption is less justified for the tight bends in a trumpet valve block). Bilbao (2011) models a valve as a junction where the pressure is equal in the main, default and bypass tubes and the volume flows sum to zero; with a partly pressed valve both paths are open and the resonances become inharmonic, which produces multiphonic sounds (Harrison et al. 2015).

## 4. The bell and radiation

### 4.1 Frequency-dependent reflection and the cutoff

Long wavelengths are reflected well before the end of the bell, and the final pressure node moves toward the bell mouth as frequency rises, so the acoustic length is shorter than the physical length by an amount that decreases with frequency (Moore 2016). The bell flare also sets the frequency above which reflection becomes negligible, which Moore calls the cutoff frequency; above it most of the energy is radiated. Benade and Jansson (1974) describe this with a "horn function" (in Benade's formulation the horn equation is recast with a potential that depends on the curvature of the bore radius, and waves are reflected where the frequency falls below that potential); this is the standard explanation, but the paper itself was not read (reference-list entry). Typical quoted cutoffs of roughly 1.2 to 1.5 kHz for a trumpet bell were not found in a primary source during this survey (unverified).

### 4.2 The Webster horn equation and its limits

Webster's equation (Webster 1919) is a one-dimensional wave equation for a tube of slowly varying cross section. It is valid when the cross section changes slowly and the diameter is small relative to the wavelength (Harrison et al. 2015). Rienstra (2005) derives it systematically as the leading order of a slow-variation perturbation problem and gives variants for bent ducts and ducts with varying sound speed and mean flow (abstract read). In the bell neither assumption holds near the mouth: the flare is rapid and the diameter reaches about 22 cm on a trombone (Smyth and Scott 2011, Table 2: bell flare from 1 cm to 10.8 cm radius over 56.7 cm). Smyth and Scott state that a one-dimensional model cannot capture the higher-order and evanescent modes in the flare. Refinements in the literature are: the Webster-Lokshin equation with a curvilinear coordinate that follows the wavefront and includes wall losses (Hélie 2003); multimodal propagation, which keeps higher-order duct modes (Pagneux, Amir and Kergomard 1996; Kemp 2002); and full numerical solutions (Noreland 2002). Eveno, Dalmont, Caussé and Gilbert (2012) compared one-dimensional models with measurement for horns and found that refinements (curvilinear horn equation, smooth connection of flared sections, spherical-wavefront radiation, wall losses) are each relevant; Bilbao and Chick (2013) and Harrison et al. (2015) cite it as showing that the one-dimensional description agrees well with measurement for trumpet and trombone. A HAL record titled "One-dimensional acoustic models of horns and comparison with measurements" has the abstract summarised here; I could not confirm that it is the same paper (abstract only).

### 4.3 Radiation impedance at the bell mouth

The usual boundary condition at the bell is the radiation impedance of an unflanged cylindrical pipe of the bell's radius (Levine and Schwinger 1948). For time-domain use it is approximated by a rational function; Silva et al. (2009) give approximation formulae. Bilbao and Chick (2013) and Harrison et al. (2015) use a passive network of two resistors, an inductor and a capacitor whose values scale with the bell radius, which keeps the scheme stable; Bilbao and Chick state that this approximation is valid up to a wavenumber times radius of about 3.8. Alternatives are a pulsating portion of a sphere as the radiating surface (Hélie and Rodet 2003) and flanged-pipe models (Dalmont, Nederveen and Joly 2001).

### 4.4 Transfer function to the far field and directivity

For synthesis, the sound heard is the flow (or pressure) leaving the bell filtered by the radiation to the listening position. Smyth and Scott (2011) measure, for a trombone, two transfer functions: one at the mouthpiece, used to couple a lip-valve model, and one outside the bell, which is convolved with the lip-valve signal to produce the sound. They estimate the bell reflection and transmission functions from measurements with incrementally changed boundary conditions (a closed 2 m tube, the same tube open, and the tube with a trombone bell) and compare them with a one-dimensional bell model built from conical segments; the comparison shows consistent behaviour but not the close match obtained for an open cylinder. Berjamin et al. (2017) use the simplest possible output: the far-field pressure as the time derivative of the bell flow, scaled by air density and divided by 4 pi times the listening distance, which treats the bell as a monopole.

Kemp, Lopez-Carromero and Campbell (2017) measured the pressure field in front of trombone bells on a two-dimensional microphone grid: the bell behaves like a spherical source at low frequencies and produces a diffracting beam at high frequencies, with an apparent source position that moves with frequency (abstract). Pätynen and Lokki (2010) recorded 14 orchestral instruments with 22 microphones in an anechoic room and report that brass instruments radiate consistently in the direction of the bell, and that playing dynamics did not change the directivity although the spectrum changed considerably (abstract). Directivity data for many instruments, including modern and historical trombones and horns, are available from TU Berlin (Weinzierl et al. 2017; Shabtai et al. 2017; Ackermann et al. 2023); see section 8.

## 5. Nonlinear propagation and brassiness

### 5.1 Observations

At fortissimo the pressure wave in a trombone steepens into a shock wave, observed both in pressure measurements and by flow visualisation (Hirschberg, Gilbert, Msallam and Wijnands 1996, abstract). Shock waves radiated from a trumpet bell have been imaged by the schlieren method (Pandya et al. 2003, cited by Smith in Physical Audio Signal Processing; details unverified). Vergez and Rodet (2001) report from artificial-mouth measurements that the spectral enrichment with level is much stronger at the bell than inside the mouthpiece, which motivates computing the nonlinear effect on the outgoing wave only. Thompson and Strong (2001) showed that including wave steepening in a model of a trombone greatly reduces the error between predicted and measured radiated spectra (abstract). Myers et al. (2012) report that the effect is present at lower dynamic levels too and contributes to the characteristic timbre of each type of brass instrument, and Norman et al. (2010) studied player control of brassiness at intermediate dynamics (reference-list entry). Rendón, Ezeta and Pérez-López (2013) argue that the strength of the effect depends on the bore shape and the mouthpiece pressure waveform, not on length alone (abstract).

### 5.2 Theory

In a plane wave of finite amplitude, parts of the waveform with higher pressure travel faster, so a compression front steepens with distance until it becomes a shock. The model equation is the generalised Burgers equation; Menguy and Gilbert (2000) give solutions for air-filled tubes including wall losses. Vergez and Rodet (2000) base their algorithm on the simple-wave equation (Burgers without dissipation or dispersion), with an added physical constraint that allows shocks (Vergez and Rodet 2001). The standard plane-wave result for the distance to shock formation is the air density times the cube of the sound speed, divided by the nonlinearity coefficient of air (about 1.2) times the maximum rate of rise of pressure (standard result of nonlinear acoustics, Hamilton and Blackstock 1998, not checked against the book here). Inference from this: the effect accumulates over distance and grows with amplitude, and the pressure amplitude of a wave falls as the bore widens, so most of the steepening happens in the long narrow cylindrical part (trumpet and trombone) and little in the bell. Reflection and transmission at junctions can be treated linearly because nonlinear propagation is cumulative and junction reflection is instantaneous (Vergez and Rodet 2001).

### 5.3 The brassiness potential parameter

Gilbert, Campbell, Myers and Pyle (2007) and Myers et al. (2010, 2012) define a brassiness potential parameter B that relates, from bore geometry alone, to how readily an instrument becomes brighter at high levels through nonlinear propagation. Myers et al. (2010) state that B depends on the bore diameters relative to the minimum (initial) bore diameter, and is computed from the minimum bore, a number of diameters measured along the length of the instrument, and the equivalent cone length. The equation itself was an image in the copy read. Inference, unverified: from that description B is the integral along the bore of the minimum diameter divided by the local diameter, divided by the equivalent cone length; check equation 1 of Myers et al. (2012) before use. Myers et al. (2012) validated B against the spectral centroid using playing tests, sine excitation and simulation, and found a trade-off between bore size and B. Musicians judge narrow-bore instruments brighter than wide-bore instruments with the same B; Myers et al. (2010) give as an intuitive explanation that a narrow bore needs a higher mouthpiece pressure for the same output level, and so produces more nonlinear enrichment. Gilbert, Menguy and Campbell (2008) give a frequency-domain simulation tool, based on generalised Burgers equations in non-uniform ducts, that predicts the brassiness from the bore.

Inference for the plugin: B gives a single number per instrument that can be checked against the nonlinear strength of a simulated bore, and it predicts the expected ordering of the four target instruments (flugelhorn and horn, being more conical, less brassy than trumpet and trombone). Specific B values are in Myers et al. (2012), not read here.

Berjamin et al. (2017) find that nonlinear propagation has a major influence on timbre and also changes the playing frequencies, the time envelopes and the playability of low notes when the lip tension is varied. They state that a complete bidirectional model with nonlinear propagation and wall losses in a tube of varying section was still a research topic.

## 6. Wall losses

### 6.1 Physics

Viscous and thermal boundary layers at the tube wall cause attenuation that grows with the square root of frequency and inversely with the tube radius, and a small dispersion. The standard frequency-domain models are those of Benade (1968), Zwikker and Kosten (1949) and Keefe (1984), who gives approximations of the transmission-line parameters for large and small radii (reference-list entries in Bilbao and Chick 2013). A commonly quoted approximation for the attenuation coefficient in air at room temperature is about 3 times 10 to the minus 5, times the square root of frequency in Hz, divided by the radius in metres, per metre (unverified, from memory). In the time domain the loss terms become fractional (half-order) derivatives; with simplification this gives the Webster-Lokshin equation (Hélie 2003; Harrison et al. 2015).

### 6.2 Waveguide implementations

In a waveguide the losses of a cylindrical segment are applied as a filter on each travelling wave, usually lumped once per segment. Abel, Smyth and Smith (2003) give a uniform approximation to the Benade and Keefe propagation constant valid over tube radii and the audio range, and derive from it a simple filter that matches the thermoviscous loss of a tube of given length and radius at a given sampling rate; its coefficients are cheap to compute, which suits tube lengths that change during performance (abstract). Smyth and Scott (2011) found measured cylinder losses agreed closely with theory and recommend a theoretical parametric loss filter such as Abel et al. for real-time use. For flared sections, Hélie and Matignon (2006) use diffusive representations of the fractional operators, and Mignot, Hélie and Matignon (2010) build discrete state-space representations of lossy flared elements arranged in a Kelly-Lochbaum structure for low-cost simulation; Mignot, Hélie and Matignon (DAFx 2010) report stability problems for convex profiles in that structure and propose a fix.

### 6.3 Time-domain grid implementations

Bilbao and Chick (2013) approximate the half derivative with an IIR filter obtained by continued-fraction expansion of the Tustin transform. At 88.2 kHz and order 20 it is accurate above about 100 Hz; an FIR approximation of the same order is poor over the whole playing range. The low-frequency error shows up as inaccurate first impedance peaks for the trombone and especially the horn, which they call a weakness of time-domain methods relative to frequency-domain ones. Berjamin et al. (2017) replace the fractional derivatives with memory variables; the error falls by about a factor of 10 when the number of memory variables is doubled, and they show results with 6. Bilbao and coauthors (2015; author list from memory, unverified) note that continued-fraction expansions converge slowly and propose passive structures optimised for accuracy at low order (abstract). Bilbao and Harrison (2016) extend passive time-domain loss models to tubes of variable cross section (catalogue record), and Harrison-Harsley (2018) proposes a passive loss model derived with electrical network theory (thesis abstract).

## 7. Modelling methods and their real-time cost

Costs below are per voice. Measured timings are from the cited papers; the operation counts are my estimates (inference) unless stated.

### 7.1 Digital waveguides

A waveguide represents each cylinder or cone as a pair of delay lines carrying travelling waves, with losses lumped into filters and scattering at junctions (Smith 1992). Cook (1991) first used waveguides for brass synthesis in TBone; Harrison et al. (2015) state that waveguide brass reached a commercial product in the Yamaha VL1 in 1994 (Russ 1994). Finer bore modelling by concatenating many waveguide-like elements is in Berners (1999), van Walstijn and Campbell (2003) and Mignot, Hélie and Matignon (2010). The bell, which has no travelling-wave solution, is handled by reflection and transmission filters. Van Walstijn and Smith (1998) model the trumpet-bell reflectance with truncated IIR filters: a length-400 FIR filter can model it faithfully, and the TIIR form reduces the cost by well over an order of magnitude (abstract). Smith's online book shows a trumpet-bell fit made of two offset exponentials and two biquads (a fourth-order IIR), matched to the impulse response and the driving-point impedance, designed with the Steiglitz-McBride method on pulse-reflectometry data (Bell Models page).

What it gets right: exact propagation in cylinders and cones, natural time variation (slide length as a delay length, valves as switched or blended delay sections), a direct place to insert nonlinear propagation, and low cost. What it does not: flared sections need fitted filters or special elements; matching a real impedance with many small cylinders is expensive (Vergez and Rodet 2001), and conical junctions with convex profiles can be unstable (Mignot et al. 2010). Estimated cost (inference): a delay of about 355 samples round trip for a 1.38 m trumpet at 44.1 kHz; 10 to 30 junctions at about 4 operations each, the same number of first- or second-order loss filters, a bell filter of order 4 to 10, and a lip update of a few dozen operations including one square root, giving about 100 to 350 floating-point operations per sample. Real time at audio rates: yes, with large margin (STK, TBone, Vergez and Rodet, Smyth and Scott all describe real-time use).

### 7.2 Reflection-function and convolution models

The bore is represented by its reflection function and the mouthpiece pressure is computed by convolving it with the past incoming waves (Schumacher 1981 for the clarinet; Adachi and Sato 1995, 1996; Vergez and Rodet 2001; Smyth and Scott 2011). Smyth and Scott note that the convolution must have a delay less than one round trip from mouthpiece to bell and back to keep the accuracy of a waveguide coupling.

What it gets right: any measured or computed bore, including the bell, with no geometric simplification. What it does not: changes of the bore during playing need a new reflection function (one per valve combination; a continuous slide needs many or an interpolation scheme, inference); nonlinear propagation does not fit inside a convolution, which is why Vergez used a hybrid. Estimated cost (inference): the reflection function of a trumpet decays over tens of milliseconds, so a direct convolution at 44.1 kHz needs thousands of multiply-adds per sample; partitioned FFT convolution with latency below the round-trip time (about 8 ms for a trumpet, 16 ms for a trombone, 26 ms for a 4.5 m horn, inference from length and sound speed) reduces this. Real time: yes for a few voices (Vergez and Rodet ran it in real time in 1997 to 2001 with MIDI control).

### 7.3 Modal (impedance-fitting) models

The input impedance is fitted as a sum of resonant modes, each a complex pole pair, and the mouthpiece pressure is the sum of the modal responses to the lip flow (Silva et al. 2014, MoReeSC; Velut et al. 2017; Guillemain, Kergomard and Voinier 2005 for real-time clarinet synthesis with digital impedance models).

What it gets right: the playing behaviour near the resonances, which is what the lips respond to, with a small number of parameters; linear stability analysis is straightforward on the same representation (Velut et al. 2017). What it does not: the mode set must be precomputed for each bore configuration (Harrison et al. 2015), so time variation such as valve gestures is awkward; there is no explicit propagating wave, so nonlinear propagation and the radiated sound need separate treatment (inference). Estimated cost (inference): 10 to 40 modes at about 8 operations each, 80 to 320 operations per sample. Real time: yes (Guillemain et al. 2005 for the clarinet).

### 7.4 Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) models

The bore is discretised on a grid in space and time with interleaved pressure and velocity points, and the whole bore, including mouthpiece and bell, is treated as one unit rather than as cylinders and cones (Bilbao and Chick 2013). Losses use fractional-derivative filters at each grid point (section 6.3), the bell uses the radiation network of section 4.3, the lips use the one-mass model of section 2.2, and valves use the three-tube junction of section 3.5 (Harrison et al. 2015). Stability requires the grid spacing to be at least the sound speed times the time step (the Courant condition), so the number of grid points is set by the sample rate (Harrison et al. 2015). Bilbao and Chick (2013) matched measured BIAS impedances of a trumpet, a trombone and a horn to under 1 percent in frequency and about 1 dB in amplitude for most extrema, running at 88.2 kHz with order-20 loss filters; the worst error was the first horn peak (3.8 percent).

Measured cost (Harrison et al. 2015, Intel Core i5-4300U, 44.1 kHz, time to compute one second of sound): trumpet of 1.3 m with one valve, 14.0 s in MATLAB, 1.97 s in serial C, 0.86 s in C with AVX vector instructions; horn of 4.5 m with three valves, 27.9, 6.12 and 3.63 s; a 6 m custom instrument with three valves, 19.2 s in C and 7.95 s with AVX. The grid is typically a few hundred points, too small for multithreading to help. Inference: at 44.1 kHz the grid spacing is about 7.8 mm, giving about 180 points for a 1.38 m trumpet, 350 for a 2.74 m trombone and 580 for a 4.5 m horn; the cost per point is dominated by the loss-filter order. FDTD gets right everything that the one-dimensional model contains, with the actual bore profile and continuous valve positions, and it gives a consistent place for nonlinear terms. Real time: trumpet-sized instruments were real time on a 2014 laptop processor; a horn was 3.6 times slower than real time. Inference: a current desktop core is several times faster, so a single horn voice may be near real time, but the margin for several voices inside a VCV Rack patch is uncertain and the method ties the grid to the sample rate.

The NESS project (Next Generation Sound Synthesis, ERC grant 279068, Edinburgh) produced this brass environment; Harrison et al. (2015) state that binaries, example sounds and input files were available for download, and the instrument file format accepts a bore as a list of position and diameter pairs in millimetres.

### 7.5 Finite-volume and finite-element models

Berjamin et al. (2017) solve the nonlinear Menguy-Gilbert model with a finite-volume scheme with flux limiters, losses by memory variables and a one-mass lip integrated by the Newmark method; 18 minutes per second of sound (section 5.4). OpenWInD (Inria, GPLv3, Python) computes input impedance and time-domain responses with one-dimensional spectral finite elements and energy-consistent time stepping, includes thermoviscous losses, several radiation conditions and valves, and can simulate brass and reed instruments (project documentation and PyPI page). These are research and design tools rather than real-time synthesis engines (inference), but OpenWInD is useful offline for computing impedances and reflection functions from bore profiles.

### 7.6 State-space and port-Hamiltonian models

Mignot, Hélie and Matignon (2010) convert Webster-Lokshin waveguide elements into discrete state-space systems in a Kelly-Lochbaum network and use model-reduction tools to lower the cost; they validated on a real trombone and describe the approach as promising for modularity, cost and accuracy (DAFx 2009 abstract). Port-Hamiltonian formulations write each component (lip, jet, pipe, radiation) with an explicit power balance so that the assembled simulation is passive and cannot gain energy numerically. Lopes and Hélie (2016) do this for the jet and lip with a pipe and a dissipative termination, using the passive-guaranteed explicit scheme of Lopes, Hélie and Falaize (2015). Darabundit and Scavone (2025) build a discrete port-Hamiltonian clarinet whose components are interconnected in a modular, energy-conserving way (search summary only). Cost depends on the dimension of the state; the brass examples found are research models, and no real-time brass synthesiser built this way was found (inference from the sources found). The value for the plugin is the stability guarantee when lip, pipe and radiation are coupled, which matters when parameters change quickly.

## 9. What a real-time model for the plugin would most likely use, and why

This section is inference throughout, based on the sources above and on the structure of the existing MPX bowed strings (waveguide strings, friction excitation, modal body, planning player).

Bore: a digital waveguide built from the bore profile as a chain of cylindrical and conical segments, with wall losses lumped per segment using the Abel, Smyth and Smith (2003) filter (one cheap filter whose coefficients follow the segment length and radius). Reasons: the trumpet and trombone are mostly cylindrical, where the waveguide is exact; the cost is a few hundred operations per sample, so many voices fit in a patch at any VCV sample rate; time variation maps directly onto the structure; and it matches the existing string code. Fractional delays (Thiran allpass or Lagrange interpolation) are needed for tuning and for the trombone slide.

Mouthpiece: a lumped cup compliance and throat inertance, or two or three short segments from a measured mouthpiece profile, with the cup volume exposed as a parameter so that lip protrusion and mouthpiece choice can be represented later.

Bell: reflection and transmission filters fitted offline. Compute the bell's reflection and transmission from its profile with a one-dimensional transmission-matrix or finite-element model (OpenWInD or ARTool, with Levine-Schwinger or Silva et al. radiation), then fit low-order IIR or truncated IIR filters (van Walstijn and Smith 1998; Smith's fourth-order trumpet-bell example). Where measurements exist (Smyth and Scott 2011 for the trombone), use them to check the fit. The transmission filter feeds a monopole radiation term (time derivative of bell flow) and, optionally, a directivity filter from the TU Berlin data.

Lips: a one-mass outward-striking valve with Bernoulli flow, jet loss in the cup, swept flow, and the contact stiffening and extra damping on closure used by Adachi and Sato. Scale mass and stiffness with the lip frequency as Adachi and Sato do (mass inversely proportional, stiffness proportional). Keep a two-degree-of-freedom option (Adachi and Sato 1996) for the high register, where the measurements show sideways or upward striking. Integrate with a semi-implicit scheme and oversample the lip and flow if needed, as the Bernoulli square root and the closure produce sharp flow pulses.

Nonlinear propagation: an amplitude-dependent time warp of the outgoing wave in the cylindrical segments (the simple-wave approach of Vergez and Rodet 2000 and Msallam et al. 2000), implemented as a per-sample variable fractional delay whose excess speed is proportional to the local wave amplitude, with the junctions kept linear. This keeps the mouthpiece pressure realistic and adds brassiness where it physically accumulates. The fallback, if this proves unstable or too costly, is Vergez's hybrid: a linear bore for the lip feedback and a nonlinear stage applied only to the radiated signal. The nonlinear section needs oversampling or band limiting because shocks generate content above the Nyquist frequency. The brassiness potential parameter can be used to check that the four simulated instruments have the expected relative brassiness.

Valves: each valve as a pair of delay sections (default and bypass) joined to the main bore by three-port junctions with the conditions of Bilbao (2011), equal pressure and summed flows, with the valve displacement blending the two paths. This keeps half-valve sounds possible without the cost of FDTD.

Player model: precompute, per note, a modal fit of the bore impedance and run linear stability analysis (Cullen et al. 2000; Velut et al. 2017) to get the lip frequency and threshold pressure for the target resonance, then plan the attack from there, in the same way the bowed strings rehearse note starts. Survey 5 covers articulation and control.

Where this choice is weakest: the flugelhorn and French horn are largely conical and the horn is long and narrow, so they need many conical segments and careful handling of convex junctions (Mignot et al. 2010), and the horn's first peaks depend on low-frequency loss accuracy (Bilbao and Chick 2013). If the waveguide cannot be made to match the horn's measured impedance closely enough, the alternatives are a reflection-function or modal model for the horn's fixed valve combinations, or an FDTD horn at reduced loss-filter order, which was 3.6 times slower than real time in 2014 and may now be close to real time on one core.

## 10. Open questions

1. Is a one-mass lip enough? The measurements show multiple lip resonances and a change of striking type with register (Yoshikawa 1995; Cullen et al. 2000; Newton et al. 2008), and Vilain et al. (2003) found a two-mass model inadequate for a replica. Which lip model gives acceptable behaviour over the full range of each instrument has to be tested.
2. Lip parameter values for human players are sparse. The available values come from artificial lips or from simulation tuning (Berjamin et al. 2017 used trial and error). Human lips have lower quality factors than artificial ones (Newton et al.). A per-note map of mass, stiffness, damping, width and rest opening for real players was not found.
3. How accurate must the bell filters be? One-dimensional bell models miss higher-order and evanescent modes (Smyth and Scott 2011), while Eveno et al. (2012) found refined one-dimensional models adequate for brass. The audible consequence for synthesis is not established.
4. Nonlinear propagation together with wall losses in a variable-section tube, with both directions of travel, was still a research topic in 2017 (Berjamin et al. 2017). How much the reflected wave's steepening matters for the lips is not settled for a real-time model.
5. Low-frequency loss accuracy: time-domain loss filters are least accurate at the lowest frequencies, which affects the first peaks of the trombone and especially the horn (Bilbao and Chick 2013).
6. Stability of conical waveguide chains with convex profiles (Mignot, Hélie and Matignon, DAFx 2010).
7. Machine-readable measured data for all four instruments were not found. Most impedances are in figures; BIAS is a commercial system; the UNSW trombone file was the only raw impedance file found. The TU Berlin directivity database carries an "In Copyright" rights statement, while the 2023 directivity set is CC BY-SA.
8. Whether to model the vocal tract upstream of the lips (Fréour and Scavone 2013) and how it interacts with the player model.
9. Wall vibration of the tubing and bell has measurable effects according to work cited by Moore (2016) (Moore et al. 2005; Kausel et al. 2015; details unverified); its size relative to the other approximations here is unknown.
10. Breath temperature and humidity shift the tuning during playing (UNSW work on warming up, not read); whether the model should include this.
11. The exact definition and published values of the brassiness potential parameter need to be read from Myers et al. (2012) before it is used as a check.
