# The brass player and existing implementations: literature survey

Compiled 13 October 2026 as survey (5) of five on real-time physical models of trumpet, flugelhorn, trombone and French horn for MPX. The other four are physics-and-methods.md, trumpet-and-flugelhorn.md, trombone.md and french-horn.md in this folder; this file covers what the player controls (Part A) and what has already been built (Part B), and cross-refers to the others for bore, lip and radiation physics.

Conventions. A statement without a marker is stated by the cited source. "Inference" marks my own reasoning or arithmetic. "Unverified" marks a fact from memory or from a secondary mention that I could not check. The reference list at the end gives the reading status of every source: read in full, read in part, abstract only, search-index excerpt only, or known only through another source. The shared web-search budget for this session ran out before the survey was finished, so several items (trombone and horn blowing pressures, brass vibrato measurements, multiple-tonguing rates) stayed as gaps; they are listed under Open questions.

## Summary

The only broad measured data set on what a brass player does with the breath is Fletcher and Tarnopolsky (1999) for the trumpet: each note has a threshold mouth pressure roughly proportional to its frequency (about 4 to 8 Pa per Hz depending on the player), the radiated level then rises about 15 dB per doubling of pressure and finally saturates at about 3 dB per doubling, and the professional reached about 25 kPa on top notes. Equivalent tables for the trombone and the horn were not found; the trombone studies from UNSW give mean pressure differences across the lips of about 1.3 kPa in normal mid-register playing.

The player selects the harmonic by setting the lip resonance near the wanted bore resonance, plays slightly above the bore impedance peak, and can bend the pitch about half a semitone up and a whole tone down on the trombone (Boutin et al. 2015, 2020). A lip slur on the horn takes 80 to 100 ms from one steady state to the next, regardless of interval or player, and the new oscillation builds up only after the first reflection returns from the bell (Logie et al. 2009). Tongued attacks show a mouth-pressure step of about 1 to 2 kPa at the release on the trumpet in mf, and a soft attack can start with a small blow of air before the lips buzz (Hruška et al. 2015). Measured brass vibrato rates and depths were not found.

Brightness with level comes mostly from nonlinear propagation in the cylindrical bore rather than from the lips (Hirschberg et al. 1996; Fletcher and Tarnopolsky 1999; Stevenson et al. 2009), and some players can change brassiness at constant level with the embouchure (Norman et al. 2010, via trombone.md). Mutes act in two ways that a model can separate: a mute inside the bell changes the reflection at the bell and adds a cavity (Helmholtz) resonance to the input impedance, and it also filters the radiated sound (Campbell, Gilbert and Myers 2021; Ancell 1960). The horn hand raises the impedance peaks near the bell cutoff and, when closed to about 95 percent of the bell opening, makes the played note jump up about a semitone because the lips can no longer sustain the weakened resonance (Wachter 2008; Dell et al. 2010).

Of existing code, the most useful pieces are: the IRCAM trumpet model, whose complete per-sample algorithm and control parameters (mouth pressure, tube length, lip damping, lip frequency) are printed in D'haes and Rodet (2002); the NESS brass environment, whose score format is a ready-made list of player controls with physical units (Harrison et al. 2016, binaries only); STK Brass and the Faust physmodels.lib brass model as minimal references with permissive licences; and OpenWInD (GPLv3) as an offline tool for bore impedance. Among commercial products, the SWAM Solo Brass manual is the most detailed public description of a virtual brass player's controls, and the Arturia BRASS manual documents an IRCAM model whose control parameters were set by an automatic optimisation, which is the closest published analogue to the MPX rehearsal approach.

## Part A. The player

### A1. Blowing (mouth) pressure

Fletcher and Tarnopolsky (1999) measured mouth pressure with a catheter in the corner of the mouth and a calibrated Bourdon gauge, and A-weighted sound level corrected to 1 m on axis, for three trumpet players (professional GC in an anechoic room; amateurs NH and KB in a living room), each playing steady notes from pianissimo to the player's fortissimo limit over the whole compass. Their findings:

- Each note has a threshold pressure, and it rises nearly in proportion to the note frequency. The fitted slope K (threshold pressure divided by frequency) was about 7 Pa per Hz for GC, 8 for NH and 4 for KB.
- Above threshold the level rises steeply, then settles at about 15 dB per doubling of pressure, and finally enters a saturation region of about 3 dB per doubling. In the middle region the level at a given pressure is nearly independent of pitch.
- GC's highest pressure was about 25 kPa, above typical systolic blood pressure (about 18 kPa). KB's maximum was about 7 kPa and NH's about 15 kPa, so the pressure range used varies strongly between players. Maximum levels for high notes were about 110 dB(A) at 1 m.
- The same player on a B-flat cornet and an A piccolo trumpet used very similar pressures and levels for the same notes.
- They cite Fiz et al. for maximum expiratory pressure of trumpet players (printed as 23 plus or minus 5, against 19 plus or minus 1 for non-players; the unit is printed as Pa, which by context must be kPa).

Approximate values read from their Figure 4 (threshold, player GC) and Figure 1 (level against pressure, GC), my reading of the plotted points:

| Note (sounding) | Frequency | Threshold pressure | Pressure and level near the soft end | Pressure and level near the loud end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| F3 | 175 Hz | about 1.6 kPa | 1.6 kPa, 63 dB(A) | about 4 kPa, 92 dB(A) |
| C4 | 262 Hz | about 2.0 kPa | 2.0 kPa, 74 dB(A) | about 6.5 kPa, 101 dB(A) |
| C5 | 523 Hz | about 4.0 kPa | 3.3 kPa, 81 dB(A) | about 12 kPa, 108 dB(A) |
| C6 | 1047 Hz | about 7.0 kPa | 6.5 kPa, 93 dB(A) | about 23 kPa, 112 dB(A) |

Their spectral data (Figure 6) used these pressures: C4 at 2.0, 3.9 and 6.6 kPa; C5 at 3.3, 6.3 and 13.0 kPa; C6 at 6.7, 11.0 and 24.0 kPa.

Their simple lip theory gives numbers a model can start from: threshold pressure equals K times frequency because the vibrating lip mass falls as tension rises (Elliott and Bowsher's finding that mass is inversely proportional to tension); lip opening amplitude about 10 to the minus 7 metres per pascal of excess pressure for a high note, saturating at little more than 1 mm; opening area proportional to the opening to the power 1.5 with a constant of about 0.05; bore input resistance at a resonance of order 10 to the 8 Pa s per cubic metre. The generator efficiency (acoustic power into the mouthpiece divided by pneumatic power) runs from about 0.1 percent for soft to about 3 percent for loud playing. They also derive a scaling rule: the spectral development of a note at frequency f and pressure p is reproduced at frequency f times a if the pressure is multiplied by a to the power three quarters, and the high-frequency extension of the spectrum grows about linearly with blowing pressure.

Bouhuys (1965), as summarised by Fletcher and Tarnopolsky: trumpet mouth pressure about 4 kPa (30 mm Hg) for high notes played pp and 13 kPa (100 mm Hg) ff; acoustic power 20 microwatts to 1 mW for low notes and 200 microwatts to 30 mW for high notes; overall efficiency 0.01 to 0.03 percent for low notes and 0.03 to 1 percent for high notes. Only one note per instrument at two dynamics was measured.

Trombone. Boutin, Smith and Wolfe (2020) report a mean pressure difference across the lips of about 1.3 kPa in normal playing of F3 and B-flat 3 on the tenor trombone (stated in their energy calculation). Boutin et al. (2015) measured ten players from B-flat 2 to F4 with pressures upstream and downstream of the lips and high-speed video (abstract only). Fréour and Scavone (2013) measured mouth and mouthpiece pressure together in trombone playing (citation only; numbers not read). I found no table of trombone pressures from pp to ff.

Horn. No accessible primary measurement of horn blowing pressure was found (also noted in french-horn.md). The horn survey cites mouthpiece force on the lips rising from about 5 N at F2 to about 40 N at F5 in mf (Hirano and Kinoshita 2026, abstract), which is a different quantity. Inference: the Fletcher threshold law should hold in form for the horn and trombone, but K has to be found by simulation of our own lip model, not taken from trumpet players.

Flow. An IRCAM and McGill study fitted an air-velocity sensor to the tuning slide of a trumpet and reported peak flows up to 950 ml per second for low fortissimo notes (HAL record hal-00541405, abstract seen only as a search excerpt; authors not confirmed). Velut, Vergez, Gilbert and Sanchez (2015) instrumented a trumpet for mouth pressure, mouthpiece pressure, lip motion phase, valve positions, rim force on the lips, flow and sound, and report that players share some trends but use measurably different strategies (abstract only).

Values used by models. The NESS brass environment's example score uses 5 kPa reached by a 1 ms ramp (Harrison et al. 2016). Adachi and Sato used 2 to 5 kPa for moderate levels and Berjamin et al. up to 20 kPa (as tabulated in physics-and-methods.md, section 2.7). STK Brass and the Faust brass model use a dimensionless pressure from 0 to 1.

### A2. Lip tension, harmonic selection, lipping and slurs

The physics of the lip valve and its resonance relative to the played note is covered in physics-and-methods.md (sections 2.1 to 2.7). The points that matter for the player:

- Harmonic selection. The player sets the lip resonance (tension and the vibrating mass that goes with it) close to the wanted bore resonance; the note then locks to that resonance. Players normally sound slightly above the bore impedance peak; Boutin et al. (2015) give about 1.1 percent above it on the trombone (abstract). Chen, Smith and Wolfe (2012) measured the vocal tract impedance of seven trumpeters and found no systematic tuning of vocal-tract resonances over the measured range; they attribute the difference from saxophonists to the greater control a brass player has over the natural frequency of the lip valve.
- Lipping. Trombonists can "lip up" about half a semitone and "lip down" about a whole tone, and the range is roughly symmetrical about the bore impedance peak rather than about the normal playing frequency (Boutin, Smith and Wolfe 2020). Moving well away from the resonance raises the share of the vocal tract in the series impedance from below 20 percent to about 40 percent.
- In a model, the lip frequency that gives the strongest oscillation is not the sounding frequency. In the IRCAM trumpet model the oscillation is strongest when the played frequency is a multiple of the tube's fundamental and the played frequency is about four thirds of the lip parameter (ratio of lip parameter to played frequency 0.74 to 0.75 over modes 2 to 10, D'haes and Rodet 2002, Table 2). This ratio is a property of that particular model; in Adachi and Sato's model the lip eigenfrequency is 1 to 8 percent above the sounding frequency (physics-and-methods.md section 2.6). Inference: the player must take its lip-frequency targets from tables computed for our own lip model.
- Fingering choice. D'haes and Rodet (2002) state two constraints that a virtual trumpeter needs: the tube length is fixed for the duration of each note, and a real trumpet offers only seven tube lengths, from which the player chooses a fingering and a mode for each note. Their table shows that some notes are available on several combinations (G4 as mode 6 of the open tube, mode 7 of the fourth tube length or mode 8 of the sixth). Their earlier automatic control estimation produced vibrato by varying the tube length, which a real player cannot do, and adding the constraint gave much better resynthesis.

Lip slurs. Logie, Stevenson, Apostoli, Chick and Campbell (2009) filmed the lips of two skilled horn players at 5000 frames per second through a windowed mouthpiece copied from a Paxman 4C, with mouthpiece and radiated pressure synchronised, on a narrow-bore and a wide-bore horn. Slurs were octaves D3 to D4 (resonances 4 to 8 of the horn in open D) and fourths C3 to F3 (resonances 3 to 4 of the open F horn), upward only. Findings:

- The time between the two steady states was 80 to 100 ms (about 0.1 s for player A, 0.08 s for player B), and it did not vary much between players, intervals or the two horns.
- The lip open area first decreases; player A then made about 15 small cycles and player B 5 or 6 before the amplitude built up again on the new resonance. During the transition the mouthpiece pressure was erratic for player A; for player B the radiated sound nearly stopped for about 30 ms, an audible break.
- Player A practised slurs slowly through the intermediate resonances and showed four distinct frequency plateaus in the transition, some on resonances and some not; player B aimed to jump as fast as possible.
- They describe the middle of a slur as similar to a starting transient: a small lip oscillation that grows after one round trip of the pressure wave. A slur took about twice the time of a starting transient. They note that at mode N the lips complete about N cycles during one round trip in the tube, so higher modes take more lip cycles to lock.

A later paper from the same group (CFA 2010, "Upward and downward slurred transients on brass instruments: why is one not simply the inverse of the other?") states that players use significantly different techniques for upward and downward slurs, most visibly over large intervals (abstract only). Hruška, Švejda and Guštar (2015) filmed a semi-professional trumpeter at 12000 frames per second: in a slow upward slur F4 to B-flat 4 the lips stopped closing fully for about 0.2 to 0.25 s and the mouth pressure oscillation grew before the change of mode, while the upper harmonics weakened before the change; downward slurs were easier, and less experienced players relaxed the lips too much, producing an audible attack inside the slur.

Valve and slide changes. Widholm (1997) studied how the valve mechanism affects the fine structure of slurs (citation only, through Logie et al.). In the NESS environment, a valve moving gradually between open and closed makes the lips fail to lock and produces multiphonic sounds in the transition (Harrison et al. 2016). Trombone slide legato is discussed in trombone.md section 5.4; no primary measurement was found there either.

### A3. Articulation: tonguing, attacks and releases

The tongue acts as a valve upstream of the lips: it seals the airway against the teeth or palate while the mouth pressure is raised, and its release starts the flow. Direct brass measurements are few.

- Hruška, Švejda and Guštar (2015), trumpet, F4 and B-flat 4, high-speed video of the lip opening synchronised with mouth pressure (2 kHz) and sound. A sharp mezzoforte attack showed an initial mouth-pressure step of almost 2 kPa, a small drop and then the normal transient after a "brassy crack"; the lips opened and closed fully from the first cycle. A very soft attack had an initial step of less than 1 kPa and began with an "initial blow off" of about 250 Pa with the lips already open, which the authors say removes the crack. At the end of a note the lips kept oscillating after the tone had stopped, which they describe as high inertia of the lips. In soft triple tonguing (ta-ta-ka-ta-ta-ka) the "ta" strokes produced clear peaks in mouth pressure and the "ka" strokes flatter ones, which they link to the known loss of sharpness when the back-tongue stroke is used badly. These are pilot results from one player.
- Iltis, Frahm, Voit, Joseph, Schoonderwaldt and Altenmüller (2015) filmed double tonguing (T-K) in six elite horn players with real-time MRI. 30 frames per second was inadequate and 100 frames per second was needed. Tongue-tip and mid-tongue movements were in antiphase, with contacts at the back of the teeth (T) and the hard palate (K); tip movement ranged over about 20 to 35 mm along the measurement line and mid-tongue movement about 10 to 20 mm; one subject's data showed a dominant frequency near 6 Hz. Note rates per subject are in a table I could not read.
- Heyne, Derrick and Al-Tamimi (2019) used ultrasound on 10 New Zealand English and 10 Tongan trombonists and found that the native language influences tongue position during sustained notes, but not the pedagogical claim that higher notes use a higher tongue (search-index excerpt only).
- Luce and Clark (1965) measured attack-transient durations of orchestral instruments from 14 to 85 ms, increasing in the order double reeds, clarinet, brass, strings, flute (abstract seen only as a search excerpt; definition of attack time not read).
- Logie et al. (2009), above, place a starting transient at about half of a slur, which by my arithmetic is about 40 to 50 ms on the horn notes they measured.

No brass study of the "tu" and "du" syllables as different tongue releases was found. The UNSW group measured tongue action, blowing pressure, mouthpiece and bore pressure and sound together for clarinet articulation (newt.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/articulations.html), which is the method a brass study would need. No measured data on releases (tongue stop against breath stop) or on maximum single, double and triple tonguing rates were found; forum figures exist but are not data.

How models implement attacks. STK Brass multiplies a maximum pressure by an ADSR envelope (default attack 5 ms; noteOn sets the attack rate from the amplitude) and adds vibrato to that pressure. The Faust brass_ui_MIDI smooths a gate with an attack time from 0 to 30 ms (default 1 ms). The NESS score gives the mouth pressure as a breakpoint function, with a 1 ms ramp in the example. Arturia BRASS offers four attack types for trumpet and trombone (direct and moderately brassy; direct and strongly marked; breathy for jazz and ballads; direct, short and breath-free for orchestral parts), with attack force taken from note velocity. SWAM Solo Brass has "Attack Tongue" (amount of percussive tonguing on mid to high velocities), "PP Attack Noise", and "Attack Control" (attack set strongly by velocity, weakly by velocity, or by the shape of the expression curve), plus "Flutter Tongue" and "Growl". None of the published descriptions models the tongue as a separate time-varying constriction upstream of the lips. Inference: a tongue model needs at least the pressure built up behind the closed tongue, the release time, and an optional residual constriction during "du" (legato tonguing); the Hruška data give a target for the pressure step and the soft-attack variant.

### A4. Vibrato

No measurement of brass vibrato rate and depth in hertz and cents was found within the search budget. What was found:

- Fletcher (2010) explains why brass players use little vibrato in orchestras: a large change of the lips' natural frequency can make the note jump to a neighbouring mode, so frequency vibrato is limited, and players can vary the lung pressure or the lip tension, which changes both amplitude and frequency.
- Lazar and Lesk list the ways a trumpeter makes vibrato: lips, jaw, throat or diaphragm, the fingertips on the valves, or the hand holding the trumpet; any of these can change the timbre. In their spectrogram comparison of three recordings of St. Louis Blues, Louis Armstrong's vibrato was the widest and least regular and Wynton Marsalis's the narrowest and most regular, with Harry James between; no numbers are given.
- The UNSW brass acoustics pages say trombonists may make vibrato by moving the slide and other brass players by lipping or rocking the instrument (search-index excerpt only).
- Implementations: STK Brass adds a sine (default 6.137 Hz, adjustable to 12 Hz) to the breath pressure, so its vibrato is a pressure vibrato; the Faust brass model multiplies the pressure envelope by 1 plus a 5 Hz sine of depth up to 0.04; NESS offers lip-frequency vibrato (a fraction of the lip frequency), tremolo on mouth pressure, and valve-opening vibrato, with 5 Hz and a lip-frequency fraction of 0.1 in its examples; SWAM models vibrato as changes to the embouchure and its contact with the mouthpiece together with the breath, separates "soft vibrato" from "shake" at half range, and has vibrato fade-in time and random rate; Sample Modeling's trumpet turns more modulation into a big-band shake.

Inference: the player needs three vibrato mechanisms (lip-frequency with some pressure modulation for trumpet, horn and flugelhorn; slide-length modulation for trombone; hand or valve vibrato as an option for trumpet), plus style rules for when vibrato is used (none or little in orchestral horn and trumpet, terminal or continuous vibrato in jazz and light music). Rates and depths will have to be measured from recordings (see Open questions).

### A5. Dynamics and timbre

- Fletcher and Tarnopolsky (1999), trumpet: spectra of individual notes have a basic envelope that rises to a maximum near 1000 Hz and then falls; louder playing extends the envelope to higher frequencies. For C4 soft and loud they describe three slopes: about 6 dB per octave rise below 1000 Hz, a near-constant region up to a transition at 2 to 3 kHz that moves up with level, and a fall of about 30 dB per octave at low level and only about 10 dB per octave at high level. The frequency at which the envelope is 20 dB below its peak rises about linearly with blowing pressure, and less for higher notes at the same pressure.
- Luce and Clark (1967), as summarised by Fletcher and Tarnopolsky: averaged over the instrument, the radiated spectrum rises 2 to 4 dB per octave below a cutoff near 1000 Hz and falls 15 to 25 dB per octave above it; with louder playing the lower slope steepens and the upper slope flattens.
- Hirschberg, Gilbert, Msallam and Wijnands (1996) observed shock waves in trombones at fortissimo by pressure measurement and flow visualisation and argue that the nonlinear propagation is essential for the sound reaching the listener but can be ignored when modelling the generation of the oscillation (abstract).
- Vergez and Rodet (2001) found on an artificial mouth that spectral enrichment with level is much stronger at the bell than in the mouthpiece; they therefore kept a linear reflection-function model for the oscillation and computed the bell sound with a nonlinear propagation algorithm.
- Stevenson et al. (2009) found that the lip open area changes little when the trombone or horn enters the brassy regime (abstract, via trombone.md). Norman et al. (2010) state that brassiness begins between forte and fortissimo, that some players can raise or lower it at constant dynamic with small embouchure changes, and that these changes alter the steepness of the pressure wavefront leaving the mouthpiece (abstract, via trombone.md).
- Myers, Pyle, Gilbert, Campbell, Chick and Logie (2012) define a "brassiness potential" computed from the bore alone, which ranks instrument families by how much they let wavefronts steepen; see physics-and-methods.md section 5.3 for values.

Inference for the player: dynamics should be specified as a mouth-pressure target per note scaled from the note's threshold, not as an output gain; the brightness that goes with dynamics then follows from the model's nonlinear propagation stage. A second, smaller embouchure control (the wavefront steepness at the mouthpiece) would give the brassiness-at-constant-level effect that Norman et al. report.

### A6. Mutes and the horn hand

Two effects of a mute can be separated in a model (Campbell, Gilbert and Myers 2021, inference in the last column):

| Mute | Effect on the air column (input impedance) | Effect on the radiated sound | Model element (inference) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight (hollow truncated cone, cork spacers) | The cavity's Helmholtz resonance adds an extra "parasitic" impedance peak; on a tenor trombone it raised resonances 2 to 4 slightly and added a peak at 77 Hz, which splits the second resonance in seventh position (E2) and makes that note unstable | Sound leaves only through the gap between mute and bell, so low frequencies are strongly attenuated | Modified bell reflection with a parallel Helmholtz resonator; output high-pass or band-pass filter |
| Cup (straight mute with a bowl almost covering the bell) | As straight mute | About 20 dB attenuation in a narrow band near 1000 Hz on a trombone, attributed to the cavity between bowl, bell and mute (Sluchin and Caussé 1991, as cited) | As straight mute plus a notch whose frequency depends on the bowl-to-bell gap |
| Harmon (cork seals the gap; stem tube in the front) | Not stated | The stem gives open-pipe resonances (Ancell 1960); covering the stem or removing it changes the timbre ("wah") | Bell termination with no leakage; output filter with tube resonances; a time-varying parameter for the hand over the stem |
| Plunger and derby (external) | Small (inference) | Plunger open: components to at least 8 kHz; closed: little energy above 4 kHz. External mutes reduce mainly the high frequencies, which radiate along the axis | Time-varying low-pass on the output; small change of bell reflection when closed |
| Historic transposing trumpet mute | Raises the modes; muted peaks fit a harmonic series about 170 cents above the open one after re-indexing | Strongly reduced | Changes the bore itself |

Further mute data. Ancell (1960) measured the first 10 to 15 partials of a cornet with several dance-band mutes: the mutes kept the same formants across notes, and all of them acted as Helmholtz resonators at 200 to 300 Hz, a range where the muted instrument's output is already low (abstract). Backus (1976) measured input impedance with and without mutes (citation only). Klaus and Pyle (2015) report that playing tests with surviving baroque trumpet mutes raise the pitch only about a semitone, although period sources say a whole tone (abstract). Campbell, Gilbert and Myers (2021) report a playing test in which a mute raised the natural notes by slightly less than a whole tone, and describe an active straight mute (Meurisse et al. 2015, as cited) that cancels the parasitic resonance with a microphone and loudspeaker inside the mute. A 2023 measurement of trumpet directivity with straight, cup and wah-wah mutes found the mute-dependent changes mainly above 1 kHz and more radiation behind the player (search-index excerpt only). A University of Illinois student report (Formosa 2010) contains measured input impedance of a B-flat trumpet with straight, cup, Harmon, plunger and practice mutes (search-index excerpt only). Practice mutes on the horn change the relative heights and the harmonicity of the impedance peaks compared with a hand (Dell et al. 2010).

The horn hand. Dell, James, Davidson and Wolfe (2010) measured an F/B-flat double horn with gelatine casts of three players' hands. The hand raises the impedance peaks, mostly near and above the bell cutoff (about 500 Hz); they cite Backus, Benade and Widholm for the finding that without the hand the peaks above 450 Hz are much smaller and disappear above 750 Hz. Different hands changed peak heights and frequencies by up to 0.6 dB and 1.3 Hz rms over peaks 1 to 20 (the abstract of the same paper gives 0.8 dB and 0.6 Hz), and the hand whose peaks were flattest was also judged flattest by seven professional players. Watts (2009) found that a hand in the bell lowered almost every resonance, most at higher modes and on the B-flat side, and made the B-flat side's partials more stable. Hand-stopping: Wachter (2008) found that as the hand closes the pitch falls continuously and the impedance peaks move without a jump, but at about 95 percent closure the played note jumps up by about a semitone, because the lips cannot sustain the now very small resonance and move to the nearest stronger one, which is usually higher; Campbell, Gilbert and Myers (2021) describe the stopped horn as an internal transposing mute giving a set of natural notes a semitone higher on the F horn. french-horn.md section 3.3 lists Wachter's stopped-horn resonance frequencies.

Inference for the player: the horn needs a continuous hand-position control acting on the bell reflection and transmission (more reflection and lower peaks as it closes), with stopping as its extreme, plus the rule that stopped notes are fingered a semitone lower on the F horn; trumpet and trombone need a mute selector with a continuous "mute control" for Harmon stem and plunger.

Two points recur. First, every system that aims at realism needs a continuous breath or expression signal per note; SWAM refuses to play live without one and warns that users tend to play too loud, recommending that most playing stay in the mp to mf range of the controller. Second, the systems add a layer of rules between the controller and the model, which is the "player": Arturia sets the model parameters for each note from tables found by an optimisation; SWAM applies "Auto Expression" during transitions, "Dynamic Pitch" (pitch change during fast expression changes), "Random Lips" (randomness in lip tuning and tension), "Legato Priority" (keeping the same valve combination or slide position when the next note allows it), valve and slide speed, half-valve contamination, release time (breath continuing after note-off), and a breath-controller mode that retriggers attacks from the breath envelope. Synful Orchestra (Lindemann) takes a different route, splicing recorded phrase fragments and resynthesising them additively ("reconstructive phrase modelling"); it is now free of charge.

### B2. Academic models with published algorithms but no released code

- IRCAM trumpet (Vergez and Rodet 1997 to 2001; D'haes and Rodet 2002). One mass with spring and damper for the lips, extra stiffness and damping when closed, a lip length that varies with small openings (tanh) to remove a discontinuity in the flow derivative at closure, a flow law that blends Poiseuille flow at small openings with Bernoulli flow at large ones, and the bore as a measured, simplified reflection function: a direct part, a run of zeros whose length sets the tube length, and a return part. Nonlinear propagation was added on the bell output path. D'haes and Rodet print the complete per-sample algorithm with four control parameters: mouth pressure, tube length, lip damping and lip frequency. It ran in real time in jMax with a WX7 or WX5. The same team inverted the model to estimate control parameters from recordings (Hélie et al. 1999; D'haes and Rodet 2001, both known through Vergez and Rodet 2001). Vergez and Tisserand (2006) discuss the playability of this model as it became Arturia BRASS (citation only).
- Adachi and Sato (1995, 1996): two-dimensional swinging lip with a reflection function (see physics-and-methods.md and trumpet-and-flugelhorn.md).
- Msallam, Dequidt, Caussé and Tassart (2000): a trombone model including nonlinear propagation, applied to synthesis of loud tones (citation only; see trombone.md).
- Berjamin, Lombard, Vergez and Cottanceau (2017; arXiv 2015): finite-volume nonlinear propagation with viscothermal losses and a one-mass lip; variable lip tension changes playing frequencies, envelopes and low-frequency playability; physics-and-methods.md gives its cost as 18 minutes per second of sound.

### B4. How musicians rate realism

Independent listening tests were not found; the evidence is reviews and forum posts. Sound On Sound praised Sample Modeling's trumpet in 2009 and the VL1's brass in 1994. On VI-Control and KVR, users who own both commonly rate Sample Modeling's brass as more convincing than SWAM's, describe SWAM brass at times as synthetic or "boxy" without equalisation and reverb, and credit SWAM with consistent tone across the range and more flexible control; one user calls Sample Modeling the only plug-in that could do jazz trumpet convincingly. These are anecdotal and partly about older versions.

### B5. What we can take from each

- STK and Faust: the smallest working loop (pressure, resonant lip filter, squared opening, junction, delay), MIT-compatible (STK) or with a licence exception for generated code (Faust). Their lip frequency is tied to the note, so they bypass the player's harmonic-selection problem; we should not copy that.
- IRCAM model: a complete, published, real-time algorithm whose structure (one-mass lip with closed-lip stiffness, smooth flow law, reflection-function bore, nonlinear output path) suits a VCV module, and whose control set (pressure, tube length, damping, lip frequency) is the core of a brass player. D'haes and Rodet's constraints (fixed tube length per note, explicit choice of fingering and mode, lip frequency from a model-specific table) are the first rules our player needs.
- NESS: the score format is a usable specification of player controls with physical units, including continuous valve opening and valve vibrato; its run times show that FDTD is real time for a trumpet but not for a horn on a 2014 laptop CPU, which favours waveguide or reflection-function bores for MPX (see physics-and-methods.md section 7).
- OpenWInD and ART: offline sources of input impedance and reflection functions for our bores. Both are GPL; MPX is MIT, so their code cannot go into the plugin, but computed data tables can be generated with them (inference; check licence terms for generated data before relying on this).
- Arturia BRASS: proof that an automatic search over model parameters per note can make a physical brass model playable from a keyboard; this is the same idea as the MPX bowed-string rehearsal and supports doing the search offline as tables plus online as rehearsal on copies.
- SWAM: the list of behavioural controls that a commercial team found necessary is a checklist for our player (attack tonguing amount, soft-attack noise, flutter, growl, fall and doit, auto expression in transitions, dynamic pitch, random lips, legato priority, valve and slide speed, half valve, release time, vibrato styles, mute control). Its idealised pipes and spectral correction controls suggest that a simplified bore plus spectral shaping can be commercially acceptable, but users' comments about a synthetic sound point the other way for us (inference).
- Sample Modeling and Synful: listeners judge realism mostly on transitions, articulation and dynamics changes, which is where the player matters more than the bore.

## What our brass player would need to control, and from what data

The bowed-string player in MPX decides from the score how each note starts, is sustained, is joined to the next and ends, and rehearses difficult starts on copies of the instrument. The brass equivalent needs the controls below. "Measured" means a source above gives values; "model" means only model defaults exist; "gap" means nothing usable was found.

| Control | What it represents | Data and source | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mouth pressure per note (target and envelope) | Breath; sets level and, through nonlinear propagation, brightness | Threshold proportional to frequency (K about 4 to 8 Pa per Hz), about 15 dB per doubling, saturation, up to 25 kPa (Fletcher and Tarnopolsky 1999); Bouhuys 4 to 13 kPa pp to ff on high trumpet notes | Measured for trumpet; gap for trombone and horn (about 1.3 kPa mean lip pressure difference in mid-register trombone, Boutin et al. 2020) |
| Lip frequency (tension) per note | Selects the harmonic; sets intonation within the resonance | Plays about 1 percent above the bore peak; lip up about half a semitone, down about a tone (Boutin et al. 2015, 2020); ratio to played frequency depends on the lip model (D'haes and Rodet 2002) | Measured relations; absolute targets must come from our own model |
| Lip damping and mass (register) | Embouchure firmness; mass falls as tension rises | Elliott and Bowsher scaling via Fletcher and Tarnopolsky; model tables in physics-and-methods.md | Model |
| Fingering or slide position and mode number | Tube length, fixed during a note; alternate fingerings and positions | D'haes and Rodet 2002 table; SWAM "Legato Priority" as a rule | Rules known; choice rules from pedagogy (unverified) |

Inference, on how these fit the MPX player design:

1. Score to gesture. For each note the player chooses fingering or slide position and mode (with legato priority across slurs), a lip-frequency target from a per-instrument table, a pressure target from the dynamic (threshold times a factor, so that the level rises about 15 dB per doubling), an attack type from the articulation (tongued sharp, tongued soft, breath attack, legato tongue, slur), a release type, a vibrato style from the genre, and mute or hand settings.
2. Tables before playing. Lip frequency and threshold pressure per fingering and mode can be found offline for each instrument by sweeping the lip frequency (the NESS paper's method) or by linear stability analysis on a modal fit of the bore (physics-and-methods.md section 2.6). This replaces Arturia's optimisation.
3. Rehearsal on copies. The brass risk at a note start is that the lips lock to the wrong mode (a split or cracked note) or lock late. A rehearsal can run candidate starts (lip frequency offset, pressure step and ramp, tongue release time) on a copy of the instrument for a few round trips, which by Logie et al. is about N lip cycles per round trip at mode N, plus margin, and check which mode captured and how fast. High horn notes, where neighbouring modes are a few semitones apart, are the case that needs it most (french-horn.md section 2.3). Copying a waveguide or reflection-function state is cheap.
4. Joins. Slurs should be planned as a lip-frequency glide plus a pressure adjustment lasting about 80 to 100 ms, with a short pressure rise before upward slurs; valve slurs need a valve travel time and the half-valve state in between; trombone joins need a decision between slide glissando, tongued legato and natural slur.

## Open questions

1. Blowing pressure against dynamic and register for the trombone and the horn: no primary table was found. Fréour's McGill thesis (2013), Fréour and Scavone (2013), Boutin et al. (2015) full text, and Bouhuys (1965) full text are the next sources to read. Failing that, we could calibrate K by matching our model's threshold behaviour to Fletcher's trumpet law and check the result by ear.
2. Brass vibrato rate and depth by instrument and style: no measurements found. We could measure them from recordings with a pitch tracker (orchestral horn and trumpet solos, jazz trumpet and trombone, brass band cornet and flugelhorn).
3. Tonguing: measured differences between "tu", "du" and "ku" releases, maximum single, double and triple tonguing rates, and tongue-stopped against breath-stopped releases on brass. Hruška et al. (2015) and Iltis et al. (2015) are pilots; the Iltis note-rate table and a 2014 ISMA paper on tonguing speed mentioned in a forum could not be read.
4. Valve travel time during fast passages and trombone slide speed and acceleration: no measurements found. Widholm (1997) is the source to obtain for valves.
5. Downward lip slurs and slurs on trumpet and trombone: Logie et al. measured upward horn slurs only; the CFA 2010 paper and Logie's 2013 thesis should be read for downward slurs and modelling.
6. Starting transients: the Edinburgh theses (Bromage 2007; Stevenson 2009) and Chick, Bromage and Campbell (2005) contain measured lip and pressure transients that could calibrate the rehearsal's success criteria; not read here.
7. Whether a single lip-frequency parameter with mass and stiffness scaled from it (Elliott and Bowsher; Adachi and Sato) is enough for the horn's high register, or whether the horn player needs a second embouchure parameter; Yoshikawa and Muto (2003) is the horn-specific source (via french-horn.md).
8. Mute models: numerical impedance or transfer-function data for modern straight, cup, Harmon and plunger mutes (Formosa 2010 report, Sluchin and Caussé 1991, Backus 1976) were not read; without them mute filters will be fitted by ear.
9. How MPX should handle brassiness at constant level (Norman et al. 2010): whether a single extra embouchure parameter is enough, and what it maps to in our lip model.
10. Licensing of tables generated with GPL tools (OpenWInD, ART) for use in an MIT-licensed plugin needs a check before relying on it.
