# Note starts without rehearsal: sources on attack control

Compiled 12 October 2026 for the note-start problem in docs/bowed-strings.md, where the model tries about 18 bow accelerations and forces on a copy of the string before each note. Each statement is marked either as stated by a source or as my inference. The reference list gives the reading status of every source: read in full, relevant chapters read, abstract only, or known only through another source.

## Summary

The published evidence does not support the idea that string players start notes cleanly by correcting the stroke within its first few milliseconds. I found no study that measured a correction of bow force or bow speed inside the attack transient, and the measured human feedback delays are longer than the interval in which an attack succeeds or fails. The fastest arm reflex appears in the muscle 20 to 45 ms after a disturbance, and force follows muscle activity by about 50 ms more. A grip-force response to a slip at the fingertips takes 74 ms. A voluntary correction appears in the muscle 120 to 180 ms after a disturbance. An auditory reaction is rarely faster than 100 ms. By my arithmetic from Guettler's analysis, three of the four conditions that decide a clean start fall within about four periods after the first slip: 5 to 18 ms on the open violin G string, 14 to 46 ms on the open cello C string. Listeners accept at most 50 ms of prolonged periods and 90 ms of multiple slipping on the open violin G string (Guettler and Askenfelt 1997). A correction driven by what the player hears or feels therefore reaches the string at or after the acceptance limit.

The sources describe preparation and safety margin instead. Players set bow force, acceleration, bow-bridge distance and hair tilt before the stroke, according to the string, the dynamic and the kind of attack; measured attacks of three violinists fall inside Guettler's triangular region, with higher force, higher acceleration and flatter hair on open strings (Schoonderwaldt 2009). Teachers describe the martelé attack, and the placing of the bow in general, as two separate events: the bow is placed and pressed into the string first, and moved afterwards (Galamian, quoted by Demoucron 2008; Fischer 2011). In spiccato the returning bow touches the still-vibrating string lightly, which damps it faster than its own losses, and then sticks to it firmly before the new stroke (Guettler 2002 thesis). The real string is also less sensitive than the models: friction-curve simulations give misleading predictions of transients, a thermal friction model performs significantly better, and string damping, torsion and finger stopping all increase the share of clean starts (Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017). Finally, clean starts are not the norm in performance: of 1694 attacks by two professional violinists, 44 percent were perfect and 80 to 90 percent fell within the acceptance limits (Guettler 2002; Guettler and Askenfelt 1997).

Bowing machines with no feedback from the string show the sensitivity the model shows, even with the string at rest before every stroke. Galluzzo's machine held force and acceleration within about 2 percent and waited 5 to 10 s between strokes; over twelve repeats of the same grid of starts the pattern of short and long transients changed, while the boundaries of the successful region stayed in place and the most reliable starts lay near its centre, and ten identical strokes in succession near the minimum force gave Helmholtz motion in three and double slipping in six (Galluzzo 2004). Lampis and colleagues report 71 to 85 percent of starts inside the playable region reaching Helmholtz motion within 20 periods, with a mean of 6.7 to 10.3 periods, about 70 to 105 ms, for those that did, on cello G strings (Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou 2024b). Both groups state that the difference between machine scatter and the reliability players experience is unexplained; Lampis and colleagues name players' modulation of force and speed during the transient as a possibility not yet tested.

Inference: the literature points to three differences between the model and a player, and none involves trying alternatives before the note. The player brings the string to a standard state before the stroke, by damping the remaining vibration and then sticking the bow to the string. The player chooses force and acceleration with margin, near the centre of the region of clean starts, which the machine data show to be the most reliable part. And the real bow-string contact is less sensitive to small differences than a friction curve is. The machine results show that a string at rest still gives scattered outcomes, so a standard starting state alone does not remove the sensitivity; the sources do not settle how much each of the three contributes.

## 1. Feedback latencies compared with the attack transient

### The attack transient (Guettler and Askenfelt 1997, read in full)

- Stimuli, as stated: a computer-controlled bowing machine on the open violin G string, 196 Hz, nominal period 5.1 ms; bow-bridge distance 28 mm, relative position 1/11.6; bow force 800 mN; each stroke started from rest and accelerated to 20 cm/s; the amount of rosin was varied between strokes. Eleven attacks spanned 160 ms of prolonged periods to 200 ms of multiple flyback. Two copies were given artificial accents (first period plus 12 dB, decaying over 5 to 6 periods). A second series was made by lowering the sampling rate to two thirds, 131 Hz, presented as "another string instrument".
- Panel, as stated: 20 advanced string students and professionals at the Norwegian State Academy of Music; each attack judged three times, 60 answers per attack; questions on acceptance (yes or no), quality and character.
- Acceptance by a 50 percent "yes" criterion, as stated: 30 to 50 ms on the prolonged side and about 90 ms on the multiple-flyback side for the violin series. For the resampled series the yes/no limits looked shorter, 60 ms for multiple flyback and none for prolonged periods, but two attacks (45 ms prolonged, 135 ms multiple flyback) fell just below 50 percent.
- Acceptance by a quality threshold (43 of 100 for the violin series, 51 for the resampled series), as stated: prolonged-period attacks shorter than about 50 ms (10 nominal periods) and multiple-flyback attacks up to 90 ms (18 periods) were accepted, with one prolonged attack of 20 ms rejected because of its slow amplitude buildup. In the resampled series the same limits in ms correspond to 7 and 12 periods.
- Preferences, as stated: quality peaked at 15 to 25 ms (3 to 5 periods) of multiple flyback rather than at the perfect attack. The authors attribute this to amplitude buildup: higher acceleration gives some initial multiple flyback but a short buildup. Buildup time to 90 percent of final amplitude ranged from 70 ms to 420 ms; the perfect attack's was 120 ms. All accepted violin attacks had buildup times under 130 ms. The authors conclude that somewhat excess bow acceleration is safer than too little.
- Character, as stated: prolonged periods are heard as choked or creaky, multiple flyback as loose or slipping. Artificial accents raised the scores of long multiple-flyback attacks by 10 and 20 units and shifted any attack toward choked.
- Scope, as stated: the limits refer to a neutral start in a neutral context, such as practising a scale. From the playing test, which covered 196 to 940 Hz, the authors suggest the limits are roughly constant in ms; for the lowest cello and double bass strings this would leave 3 to 6 and 2 to 4 periods. They say a dedicated study would be needed, and note that poor attacks were rated less harshly in the lower-pitched series.

### When a start succeeds or fails (my arithmetic from Guettler 2002 and 2004)

- Guettler (2004 review, read in full) names four instants after the first slip at which the friction force must stay below the limiting static friction (A and D) or exceed it (B and C) for slipping to repeat once per period from the start. With beta the relative bowing position: A comes at one period minus beta periods, B at one period, C near one over beta minus one periods, and D near one minus beta, divided by three beta, periods.
- Guettler (2002, read in full) reports that bow acceleration and bow force are the operative parameters while Helmholtz motion forms, and that his equations, derived for a loss-free flexible string with a rigid point bow at integer ratios, are not directly transferable to realistic models, although the triangular pattern is still recognisable. Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou (2024a) report that condition C had little effect in Guettler's simulations except at the largest bow-bridge distances.
- My numbers, counted from the first slip, which itself comes some time after the bow starts to move:
- Open violin G, period 5.1 ms, beta 1/11.6: A 4.7 ms, B 5.1 ms, D 18 ms, C 54 ms. The acceptance limits of 50 and 90 ms equal 9.8 and 17.6 periods.
- Open viola C, period 7.6 ms, beta 0.1: A 6.9 ms, B 7.6 ms, D 23 ms, C 69 ms. The limits equal 6.5 and 11.8 periods.
- Open cello D, period 6.8 ms, beta 0.08: A 6.3 ms, B 6.8 ms, D 26 ms, C 78 ms.
- Open cello C, period 15.3 ms, beta 0.1: A 13.8 ms, B 15.3 ms, D 46 ms, C 138 ms. The limits equal 3.3 and 5.9 periods.

### Auditory loop

- Pain and Hibbs 2007 (abstract only): simple auditory reaction time is thought to be rarely under 100 ms; in instrumented sprint starts the neuromuscular part of the reaction could be under 85 ms and EMG latencies under 60 ms.
- Landry and Champoux 2017 (abstract only): musicians had faster simple auditory, tactile and audio-tactile reaction times than non-musicians. I could not read the values.
- Chen, Woollacott, Pologe and Moore 2008 (abstract only): eight skilled cellists, one note per second, showed position changes within single notes that the authors interpret as pitch corrections; without the bow, and so without acoustic feedback, these within-note corrections decreased significantly. No latency is given in the abstract.
- Furuya and Soechting 2010 (abstract only): in six pianists, a perturbed tone (delayed, or altered in pitch or loudness) changed the timing and velocity of the following keystrokes.
- Repp 1999 (abstract only): removing auditory feedback had significant but very small effects on expressive piano performance; pianist listeners picked out the performance played without feedback in 63.5 percent of forced-choice trials.
- Inference: the auditory corrections documented in musicians act on later notes or on the remainder of a long note, on a time scale of hundreds of ms, not within the first 50 ms of a note.

### Proprioceptive loop (arm)

- Pruszynski, Kurtzer and Scott 2008 (abstract only): after a mechanical perturbation of the arm, the short-latency response R1 occupies 20 to 45 ms, the long-latency responses R2 and R3 45 to 105 ms, and the voluntary response 120 to 180 ms. These are times of muscle activity (EMG).
- Pruszynski, Kurtzer, Lillicrap and Scott 2009 (abstract only): R2 50 to 75 ms, R3 75 to 105 ms, early voluntary corrections 120 to 180 ms.
- Kurtzer, Pruszynski and Scott 2008, and Pruszynski and Scott 2012 (abstracts only): the long-latency response is shaped by the task and by an internal model of limb dynamics, much as voluntary control is.
- Cavanagh and Komi 1979 (abstract only): the delay between EMG onset and measurable tension in the elbow flexors was 49.5 ms in eccentric, 53.9 ms in isometric and 55.5 ms in concentric contraction; the abstract cites a general range of 30 to 100 ms.
- Inference: a reflex change of force at the hand arrives about 70 ms or more after the disturbance (20 ms to the muscle plus about 50 ms to force), and a voluntary change after about 170 ms.

### Cutaneous loop (fingers and hand)

- Johansson and Westling 1987 (abstract only): during precision grip, the delay from a small slip of the object to an increase of the grip-to-load force ratio was 74 plus or minus 9 ms, about half the minimum latency for intended grip changes triggered by cutaneous stimulation, which the authors take to show an automatic response. Fast-adapting type I and II and slowly adapting type I tactile units all signalled the slips. Adjustment to a new friction condition appeared 0.1 to 0.2 s after the object was first gripped.
- Cole and Abbs 1988 (abstract only): grip force increased 60 to 90 ms after a load increase on a held object; responses lasted 100 to 200 ms and did not adapt.
- Askenfelt and Jansson 1992 (abstract only): vibration levels on double bass, violin, guitar and piano during playing are well above vibrotactile detection thresholds at almost every position measured; finger forces in bowed-string playing were studied briefly, and the authors conclude that kinesthetic forces may assist timing.
- Chafe 1993, as described by O'Modhrain 2000 (chapter read; Chafe not read): accelerometers on the fingernails of a cellist's bowing hand recorded a burst of low-frequency vibration at note onset that disappeared once the string settled; Chafe concluded the bursts may give timing cues, and O'Modhrain adds that they may signal instability of the bow-string contact.
- Guettler and Askenfelt 1997 (read in full): the authors write that a player is in a slightly better position than a listener to decide which corrections to make, because information on the bow-string interaction reaches the bow and fingertips through the rapidly changing friction forces of the attack. The passage concerns classifying an attack so as to adjust bow acceleration or force; it does not say whether the adjustment happens within the same stroke.
- Baader, Kazennikov and Wiesendanger 2005 (abstract only): in violinists, finger placement and bow reversal were offset by minus 12 to 60 ms, and these offsets were not perceived.

### Comparison (inference)

- On the violin G string the points at which a start succeeds or fails (A, B and D) occur 5 to 18 ms after the first slip. The fastest documented sensory-driven force change at the hand needs about 70 ms (spinal reflex plus electromechanical delay, or the 74 ms slip response). No sensory loop is fast enough to act on those events.
- A correction could in principle shorten a transient that has already failed. It would act around 70 to 180 ms after the failure, which is at or beyond the 50 ms limit for prolonged periods and around the 90 ms limit for multiple slipping on the violin. On the cello, where the limits leave 3 to 6 periods, the margin is smaller still in periods; Guettler and Askenfelt note that lower instruments then need bowing parameters closer to the ideal.

## 2. Measured bow control during the attack

- No source I found measured bow force or bow speed inside the first 50 ms of an attack with enough resolution to show a correction, and none reports one. Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou (2024a) list "the ability of musicians to modulate bow force and velocity during transients" as a topic for future work.
- Askenfelt 1986 and 1989 (abstracts only) and Askenfelt 1988, a KTH report whose title, method and list of bowing patterns match the 1989 paper (read in full). As stated in the 1988 report: bow velocity was obtained from the position signal through a 10 ms RC network, resolving components up to 16 Hz; bow force was band-pass filtered and smoothed with a 3 ms time constant. A sforzando was played with up to about 1.4 m/s bow speed at about 10 mm from the bridge, with the level rising at up to about 100 dB per second, then slowed to 0.1 m/s at 20 mm. Players kept a safety margin above minimum bow force throughout the dynamic range, larger for whole notes. In one crescendo near a bow change the bow came to about 4 mm from the bridge and was then moved away while force stayed high; the author offers two readings, a correction after the player sensed loss of control or an accidental overshoot corrected at once. This happened over the course of a crescendo, not within an attack. Inference: a 16 Hz velocity bandwidth cannot show changes inside the first 50 ms.
- Guettler and Askenfelt 1997 (read in full): the playing test recorded string velocity and sound only, not bowing parameters.
- Guettler 2002 (read in full): from a simulation of a perfect attack, to keep regular triggering the limiting static friction force should fall by no more than about 0.3 percent per period over the first seven periods, and no more than 0.9 percent per period over the next eight, so the post-attack reduction of bow force must be made with care. In his closing note he states that a player controls the bow's acceleration not only with the arm but with the firmness of the bow hold and wrist, which many players find effective near the bridge, where acceleration must be kept smooth early in the transient.
- Schoonderwaldt 2009 thesis (summary chapters read): three violinists played repeated notes with pauses so that the string was at rest before each attack; soft, neutral and strong attacks, three dynamic levels, all strings open and stopped, 432 attacks per session. Bow acceleration and force were averaged over the first 10 ms after onset. On the stopped D string (162 attacks) the force-acceleration combinations formed triangular regions consistent with Guettler's criteria, rotating with bow-bridge distance as Guettler predicts. Players used higher bow force and acceleration on open strings, which the author reads as greater tolerance for the more critical open string, higher forces on lower strings, and flatter hair (less tilt) for strong attacks and on open strings. The author states that analysis of attack quality and of bow control during the attack was still needed. Schoonderwaldt 2009 in JASA (abstract only) analyses only the steady part of notes.
- Demoucron 2008 thesis (relevant chapters read): in fast martelé measured on a professional violinist, bow force rose to about 1.5 N while the bow was at rest between strokes and fell to about 0.3 N within about 0.2 s once it moved, with ripples near 13 Hz that the author attributes to the bow responding to the sudden release of finger force; an advanced non-professional showed much larger ripples. To control his violin model, Demoucron chose each attack's force and acceleration inside the simulated Guettler triangle, open loop. He also found that in one of three test sets the outcome of an attack in his model changed with the computation rate (a regular start at 441 kHz, a non-periodic one at 44.1 kHz); in the other two sets the rate made no significant difference.
- Maestre, Blaauw, Bonada, Guaus and Pérez 2010 (abstract only, through a search summary): bowing-parameter contours of measured notes are represented as Bézier segments with Gaussian-mixture statistics and regenerated by a bow-planning algorithm to drive a waveguide model, open loop. I found no analysis of corrections during attacks in this work.
- Galluzzo 2004 thesis (relevant chapters read), Galluzzo and Woodhouse 2014 (abstract only), Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017 (read in full). As stated: the machine bowed the open D string of a cello, first with a rosin-coated perspex rod and later with a real bow; constant force, constant acceleration from rest. The thesis gives force and acceleration control within about 2 percent and a speed-controller response of 0.01 s; the 2017 paper gives about 10 ms for changes of acceleration and force constant within 3 percent. A Guettler diagram at beta 0.08 was measured twelve times: the border of the successful region stayed fairly fixed while the detailed pattern of short and long transients varied chaotically, which Galluzzo attributes to imperceptibly small differences in conditions. Combining the twelve, the region where even the longest transient was under 20 periods narrowed and the region where the shortest was under 20 periods widened, so the most reliable operating points lie near the centre of the region. Four repeats with a real bow showed the same degree of repeatability. Ten strokes in immediate succession under identical conditions near the minimum-force limit gave Helmholtz motion in three (and at the very end of a fourth) and double slipping in the other six, and no two waveforms were alike in every detail. Before each stroke the machine lifted the bow for half a second so the string could return to equilibrium, replaced it, and waited 5 to 10 s, so the string was at rest at every start. Repeating the diagram seven times in reverse order, to test whether the previous stroke influenced the next through slow thermal effects, did not change its general appearance.
- Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017 add, as stated, that the speckled texture of the measured diagrams may surprise a string player, since a cellist experiences the string's response as fairly reliable and repeatable, and that it is not clear that all transients failing their criteria are unacceptable in practice.
- Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou 2024a (read in full): an industrial robot arm bowed a cello G string on a rigid monochord at beta 0.0786, 30 accelerations by 33 forces, success defined as Helmholtz motion within 20 periods (0.204 s). The playable region was triangular, with no playable starts below about 1 N; failed starts occurred inside it in a speckled pattern; perfect starts formed no clear cluster. Six repetitions, in ascending and descending order and a month apart after remounting the string, kept the size and shape of the region. The authors state that the speckled diagram fails to capture the ability of experienced players, who in their words achieve perfect transients almost every time. That claim is not supported by data in the paper and conflicts with the 20 to 50 percent measured by Guettler and Askenfelt.
- Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou 2024b (read in full): four types of cello G string, six repetitions each. Within the playable region the share of successful starts was 71.4, 85.0, 70.7 and 77.6 percent (plus or minus 3 to 5), and the mean length of successful transients was 10.3, 6.7, 9.0 and 7.4 periods. At about 98 Hz these are 105, 69, 92 and 75 ms (my conversion). The authors note that 20 periods exceeds what players accept.
- Pàmies-Vilà, Mayer, Matusiak and Chatziioannou 2024 (abstract only): a robot arm replaying the motion-captured bow trajectory of an experienced cellist reproduced bow velocity accurately, but minimal variations in position compromised bow-string contact. Inference: the cellist's contact force is not determined by the arm's trajectory alone; it depends on the compliance of hand, bow and hair.
- Inference on the question whether machine attacks fall in Guettler's diagram as cleanly as players' do: no study compares the two on the same instrument. The machine studies show a repeatable wedge with unrepeatable detail; the only measured player statistics (Guettler and Askenfelt) show many imperfect but acceptable starts. The difference reported by the researchers is between machine scatter and players' experience, and has not been measured directly.

## 3. Mechanisms that make attacks robust without feedback or rehearsal

### Starting with the bow on the string

- Guettler 2002 (read in full) lists three ways to start a stroke: from the air (speed but no force), from the string (force but no speed), and from zero speed and zero force. From his experience as a professional player and teacher he states that violinists often start a singing phrase from the air, while cellists and double bassists more often start with string contact, even for gentle onsets, because starting noise lasts longer on lower instruments. Accomplished players keep the bow on the string between strokes in a phrase, and students spend much time practising clean onsets of separate tones with the bow in full contact. His analysis concerns starts from the string with constant force and constant acceleration from rest.
- Guettler 2006 (read in full): in martelé the stroke starts on the string with high force, giving slightly prolonged initial periods; if this lasts less than about 20 to 30 ms the attack sounds accented, not noisy. In spiccato the first slip should occur only when bow force is close to its maximum. Guettler 2002 thesis (read in full): in a clean spiccato the bow changes direction while in contact with the string and the release comes just before bow force peaks; if the bow changes direction in the air and lands with substantial speed, multiple slips occur at every onset. Guettler 2010 (read in full): in good crisp spiccato the bow change takes place after the bow has landed, contrary to what players generally believe.
- Galamian, quoted in Demoucron 2008 (Galamian not read directly): the martelé accent requires a preliminary pressure applied before the bow moves, a "pinch" stronger than the stroke needs, lessened immediately once the stroke begins; released too early there is no accent, too late a scratch.
- Fischer 2011 and Fischer, Martelé (read in full): before the stroke the hair grips the string extra hard, giving a click at the start; this "bite" is made during the gap between strokes. As an exercise he describes pressing the bow into the string without moving it and pushing the string sideways without the hair slipping. He names the commonest martelé fault as confusing the moment of gripping the string with the moment of moving the bow, and generalises this as technical timing preceding musical timing.
- Demoucron 2008 (read): measured pinch force of about 1.5 N at rest before each fast martelé stroke (see section 2).
- Inference: in the starts that teachers describe, the bow is stuck to the string under force before it moves, so each start begins from the same kind of state: the string held at the bow, the bow at rest, and a known force. Guettler's diagram describes exactly this kind of start.

### A string still vibrating from the previous note

- Guettler 2002 thesis (read in full), on spiccato: after the bow leaves the string the vibration decays exponentially; then the returning bow, touching lightly without disturbing the triggering pattern, damps the string faster than its own losses ("forced decay"); then, as force rises, the bow takes a sudden firm hold that quickly restrains what remains of the Helmholtz corner, so that it does not interfere with a new pattern of the opposite rotation.
- Guettler 2006 (read in full): in ricochet the string should vibrate freely between attacks because the corner continues in the same direction and is only refreshed; in spiccato it should be well damped because the rotation must reverse at each stroke.
- Guettler and Askenfelt 1997 (read in full): the second of two consecutive up-bows was played perfectly in 12 successive recordings by both players; the authors explain that the Helmholtz corner was already circulating in the direction the second stroke needs, so no reversal of the string motion had to take place. Their figure 9 shows an excellent ricochet attack with a decaying string vibration, mainly the fundamental, present at its onset.
- Pitteroff and Woodhouse 1994 and 1995 (abstracts only): reinterpreting Cremer's measurements of transverse waves meeting a bow at rest, both the width of the bow and the elasticity of the hair have a large effect on how waves are reflected and transmitted at the bow.
- Inference for the model: in bow-changes.md the model's resting bow holds the string rigidly at the contact point and the two segments ring on almost undamped. The sources describe a real bow at rest as neither rigid nor a point, and describe players damping the residual vibration with a lightly touching, sliding bow before taking a firm hold. A light sliding contact dissipates energy through friction; a rigid stuck contact does not. Against this, the machine studies in section 2 started every stroke from a string at rest and still found scattered outcomes, so residual vibration is not the only source of the sensitivity.

### Bow-hair compliance and width

- Pitteroff and Woodhouse 1995 (abstract only): the differential slipping caused by finite bow width depends strongly on hair compliance, and suitable tilting of the bow reduces its harm to the establishment of Helmholtz motion. Pitteroff and Woodhouse 1998 Part III (abstract only, through a search summary): modelling the finite width markedly changes the region of the player's control space that gives acceptable waveforms.
- Guettler 2006 (read in full): violinists usually tilt the hair about 30 degrees; this reduces the effect of small bowing inconsistencies and facilitates gentle attacks and attacks near the bridge; he cites Pitteroff and Woodhouse 1998 as showing in simulation that correct tilting increases the safety margins of the Helmholtz regime.
- Guettler 2002 (read in full): at non-integer bowing positions the local fluctuations of the start pattern might be less observable when the hair ribbon covers a length of string. Guettler 2004: the full hair width of about 8 mm covers at least 2.4 percent of a violin string when flat.
- Mansour, Woodhouse and Scavone 2016 (read in part), cello bow values compiled from Gough and other sources: about 290 hair strands, about 50 in contact with the string; longitudinal impedance of the ribbon about 10 kg/s, first longitudinal resonance about 1950 Hz; tension 70 N, first transverse resonance 75 Hz, transverse impedance 0.79 kg/s; Q about 20 transverse and 10 longitudinal, with damping dominated by friction between strands. Cello string transverse impedances are 0.4 to 1.1 kg/s, close to the hair's transverse value and an order of magnitude below its longitudinal value.
- Galluzzo 2004 (read): the speckled pattern was the same with a real bow as with the rigid rod, so hair compliance alone does not remove it.

### Compliance of the hand and arm

- Guettler 1997, Bow notes (read in full): preliminary experiments holding a 60 g mass in a bow grip showed the hand influences the total impedance significantly only below about 100 Hz, and is mainly resistive below 20 to 30 Hz. In ricochet the player removes rotational energy from the stick by yielding to the frog's movement rather than resisting it.
- Galluzzo 2004 (read): with a real bow, the machine's force controller had a closed-loop bandwidth of about 6 Hz and left the bow's bouncing mode undamped; a light frictional contact between the bow tip and paper was added, bringing that mode near critical damping and keeping force fluctuations under 3 percent.
- Demoucron 2008 (read): the professional's force traces in martelé were much smoother than the advanced amateur's (see section 2).
- Inference: the hand acts as a damper of the bow's low-frequency modes and sets the stiffness of the bow hold, which Guettler says shapes acceleration. It is not a fast feedback path at the string's frequencies.

### Torsion and damping

- Guettler 2002 (read in full): torsional Q values measured by Woodhouse and Loach for cello D strings are 34 (steel), 46 (nylon) and 20 (gut); torsion adds losses at reflections from the bow, which partly reduces the cancelling effect of the initial slip pulse at instant C, and torsional ringing makes his simple equations unsuitable as predictors. Every string player knows attacks are harder on open strings than on fingered ones; a fingered string decays much faster; cellists and bassists often pluck an open string lightly with the left hand during a bowed attack.
- Schoonderwaldt 2009 (read): larger damping rounds the Helmholtz corner, so its echoes are weaker and less likely to start extra slips; stopping the string greatly increases damping.
- Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017 (read in full), simulations with the classical friction curve: a finger-stopped string (internal damping raised to measured values) gave more successful starts and shorter transients; a rigid bridge gave fewer and longer; leaving out torsion gave a surprisingly small number of successes; leaving out bending stiffness gave more successes, clustered more densely around the centre of the wedge. They state that a denser clustering is likely to correspond to a note that is easier to play.

### Friction model

- Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017 (read in full): measured transients were compared with two friction-curve models and a thermal model. The thermal model performed significantly better. Friction-curve models produce jumps in force and velocity that lead to "twitchiness", and the authors conclude that friction-curve models of any kind give misleading predictions of transient response. The simulated diagrams with friction curves show radial lines of similar transients that the measurements do not show. Earlier work found the thermal model more benign: Helmholtz motion formed faster and more reliably. They also state that no current model is physically complete; the thermal model rests on one averaged contact temperature, while rosin's shear modulus changes by about five orders of magnitude between room temperature and 70 degrees C.
- Galluzzo 2004 (read): a real cello behaving like the friction-curve simulation would be extremely unplayable, requiring unreasonable precision; in friction-curve simulation, small changes such as a small reduction of the sampling rate can start a slip that otherwise would not occur.
- Woodhouse 2003, as summarised in bow-changes.md: Helmholtz motion established more readily, with shorter and more regular transients, than with the friction curve.

### Choice of operating point

- Galluzzo 2004 (read): the most reliable starts are near the centre of the region, the least reliable near its edges.
- Guettler and Askenfelt 1997 (read): on the open E string players often use excess bow force or lower speed as a safety margin against long multiple slipping, to which thin strings are prone; this habit appeared on the stopped E string too, giving prolonged periods.
- Schoonderwaldt 2009 (read): higher force and acceleration on open strings, flatter hair (see section 2).
- Askenfelt 1988 (read): a safety factor above the minimum bow force throughout the dynamic range.
- Guettler 2004 (read): players choose the starting point on the hair ribbon so that the dynamics of the bow shape the force envelope of the attack.

### Sensitivity as a property of the system

- Schumacher and Woodhouse 1995 (abstract only): systematic simulation maps the basin of attraction of Helmholtz motion in a subspace of the player's control space; its size and structure vary with the parameters of the string and instrument.
- Pégeot, Colinot, Doc, Fréour and Vergez 2024 (abstract only): for self-sustained instrument models, regime selection and transient duration depend on initial conditions as well as control parameters; they propose computing the probability of each regime and the probability distribution of transient durations over a distribution of initial conditions.
- Galluzzo 2004 (read), describing Woodhouse 1993: with the string starting from rest, the least bow force that led to Helmholtz motion was almost two orders of magnitude above Schelleng's steady-state minimum.

### Motor control without feedback

- Keele 1968 (not read; citation checked): commonly cited as the source of the definition of a motor program as a set of muscle commands prepared before a movement sequence begins and carried out without being influenced by peripheral feedback. I have not checked the wording against the paper.
- Schmidt 1975 (abstract only): after finding closed-loop accounts of motor learning inadequate, proposes a schema theory in which a recall memory produces the movement and a recognition memory evaluates whether it was correct afterwards.
- Harris and Wolpert 1998 (abstract only): if motor noise grows with the size of the command, trajectories are planned to minimise the variance of the outcome; this predicts observed arm and eye movements.
- Todorov and Jordan 2002 (abstract only): under stochastic optimal feedback control the best strategy allows variability in directions that do not affect the goal and corrects only deviations that do.
- Burdet, Osu, Franklin, Milner and Kawato 2001 (abstract only): people learn to stabilise unstable dynamics, such as keeping a screwdriver in a slot, by selectively controlling the mechanical impedance of the arm.
- Inference: these accounts describe skilled fast actions as prepared in advance, with feedback acting on the next attempt or on slower components, and with stiffness chosen beforehand to resist disturbances. In terms of these accounts an attack would be a prepared action whose parameters are learned per string and per kind of attack, with the outcome evaluated afterwards.

### String pedagogy

- The pedagogical sources found describe the attack as preparation: placement of the bow, a preliminary pressure, and the timing of its release (Galamian through Demoucron; Fischer 2011; Guettler 2002 on practising clean onsets with the bow on the string; Guettler 2010 on spiccato). The faults they name are faults of preparation or timing, such as moving at the moment of gripping or releasing the pressure too early or too late. None of the sources I read describes listening to the start of the note and correcting it during the stroke.

## 4. Haptic bowing controllers and feedback latency

- O'Modhrain 2000, chapter 5 (read): a two-axis force-feedback device (the Moose) controlled a waveguide bowed-string model over MIDI; normal and friction forces were computed separately in a Dahl-type friction model, because the haptic and audio models could not exchange data at audio rate. Twenty novices imitating a recorded stroke scored about the same with friction (mean 4.50) and without it (4.40), on a 1 to 7 scale where 1 is most similar to the target stroke. Six experienced string players scored worse with friction (5.4) than without (4.5) but mostly preferred the friction; O'Modhrain reads this as negative transfer from an unrealistic friction model. No latency figures are given.
- Nichols 2000 and 2002 (read): the vBow senses bow motion with encoders and drives servomotors that render velocity-dependent friction and randomly varied vibration, not forces computed by the string model. Latency is mentioned as a problem of MIDI violins and of the Windows operating system, to be reduced by moving to RTLinux; no figures are given.
- Schoonderwaldt, Sinclair and Wanderley 2007 (read): interpret O'Modhrain's result to mean that the net resistance felt by the hand may matter more than the detail of the friction, much of which is filtered out by the bow.
- Schoonderwaldt 2009 thesis (read) states that haptic experiments have shown such feedback facilitates control of virtual instruments, citing O'Modhrain, Nichols and Florens. O'Modhrain's own results do not show a benefit; Florens 2003 was not read.
- Sinclair 2012 thesis (relevant sections read): a single-axis friction display running at 40 kHz; six participants adjusted noise and delay in the velocity signal until they detected distortion. The detection threshold for added delay fell with friction level; at the highest friction, 2.5 N, the median was 0.3 ms (12 samples), and some participants detected 2 or 3 samples. This concerns the fidelity of the simulated contact, a mechanical loop, not the player's sensorimotor loop.
- Beigi and Conneely 2026 (arXiv preprint, abstract only, not peer reviewed): in a finite-difference bowed-string model, a recurrent learned bow controller outperformed a feedforward one when bow speed was changed mid-stroke, but no learned controller improved on the lookup rule that generated its training labels where that rule was correct.
- Inference: none of the haptic bowing work found measures how the delay of the human feedback loop affects attack quality. The only latency numbers concern rendering the contact itself, where delays well under 1 ms are perceptible.

## 5. Real attacks are imperfect and accepted

- Guettler and Askenfelt 1997 (read in full), playing test: two professional violinists, simple tune in tenuto, détaché and martellato at piano, mezzo forte and forte on the G and E strings; 1764 attacks recorded, 1694 (96 percent) classified; attacks with less than 5 ms of aperiodic start counted as perfect.
- Between 20 and 50 percent of attacks were perfect, depending on dynamic and bowing style; the highest rate, 79 percent, was the second player's martellato at piano. Between 80 and 90 percent stayed within the 50 and 90 ms limits. Of 121 renderings of the 14-note tune only one had all 14 attacks perfect, although informally a professional could produce a sizeable series of perfect attacks when concentrating on it.
- Guettler 2002 and the 2002 thesis report 44 percent of the 1694 attacks as perfect; the 2004 review states more than 50 percent. I could not resolve the difference.
- Long multiple-flyback starts of 100 to 600 ms occurred, sometimes lasting the whole note, mainly in tenuto and détaché at piano; pitch was still heard correctly, and such notes sound like cautious light bowing rather than a poor attack.
- At piano only perfect and multiple-flyback attacks occurred; martellato at mezzo forte and forte had many prolonged-period starts; on the E string the distribution shifted toward prolonged periods, with the martellato peak at 10 to 20 ms of prolongation.
- Spiccato on one string: 64 percent perfect, falling to 33 percent when a second string was interleaved. Sautillé: 35 percent perfect, with prolonged starts up to 100 ms. Ricochet: 45 percent perfect, but almost 20 percent of sixteenth notes (70 to 100 ms long) spent most of their length in the transient.
- The authors state that transients well outside the limits occur in professional performance, that not all professionals reach the same quality, and that departures are sometimes intended, such as sforzandi of prolonged periods or a section entering softly with seconds of multiple slipping.
- Guettler 2002 thesis (read), citing Backhaus 1932 through Cremer: in a violin A4, the five lowest harmonics took more than 100 ms to reach full amplitude, and the fundamental built up later than the next partials, which Guettler reads as irregular triggering for more than 95 ms.
- Guettler 2006 (read): prolonged periods shorter than about 20 to 30 ms in martelé sound accented rather than noisy.
- Galluzzo, Woodhouse and Mansour 2017 (read) and Lampis, Mayer and Chatziioannou 2024b (read): the 20-period success criterion used for machines is longer than players accept, and the authors are not sure that all starts failing stricter criteria would be unacceptable in practice.
