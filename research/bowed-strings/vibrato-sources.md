# Vibrato on bowed strings: what is measured

Gathered for phase 7 of docs/bowed-strings.md, the player. What a vibrato is, how it varies, and when players use it.

## Rate and width

Geringer and Allen 2004, "An analysis of vibrato among high school and university violin and cello students", Journal of Research in Music Education, doi 10.2307/3345438. Forty violinists and cellists, an eight-bar passage with and without vibrato. Mean rate about 5.5 Hz, no difference between instruments or between high school and university players. Violin vibrato wider than cello vibrato. The pitch goes both above and below the note, not only one side of it. Numbers read from abstract excerpts; the full text is behind a paywall.

Meyer 1992 (as cited by Hu et al. 2025): depth 0.2 to 0.35 semitones. A common summary elsewhere: typical violin extent about 30 cents, ranging from 10 to 60.

Hu, Kim, Kim, Mackay and Heller 2025, "Influence of string register locations on vibratos among violoncellists", arXiv 2512.18162. Cello. Rate: commercial recordings mean 6.475 Hz (SD 0.42, n 54); TinySOL library recordings mean 5.261 Hz (SD 0.28, n 40). Width, half the peak-to-peak range, grows with how far the finger is from the nut, quadratically: combined fit width = 63.8 (x − 0.054)² + 3.31 cents, x the stopping point as a fraction of the string from the nut (R² 0.51); about 10 cents at x 0.38 and 17.5 at x 0.53. Rate rises a little with register in the commercial recordings (Spearman 0.37), not significantly in TinySOL.

MacLeod 2008, "Influences of dynamic level and pitch register on the vibrato rates and widths of violin and viola players", Journal of Research in Music Education 56(1). Forty high school and university violin and viola players. Reported (via later citations; the paper itself not read): considerably wider vibrato in higher registers, faster in seventh position than first. Its dynamics results not seen.

A 2010 pilot study of four professional violinists (University of Illinois listing; authors not confirmed): dynamic level and register both affected width; dynamic level affected rate, register did not.

Mick 2025, double bass: wider and faster in higher positions (as cited by Hu et al.; no numbers seen).

## How it varies within a piece

Timmers and Desain 2000, "Vibrato: questions and answers from musicians and science", Proceedings of the Sixth ICMPC. Five professionals (cello, oboe, tenor, theremin, violin) playing the first phrase of Saint-Saëns' Le Cygne at 60 bpm; per-note mean rate and extent. Extent smaller at the start and end of phrases for cello and oboe, smaller at the end for violin. Extent correlated with loudness for violin (r 0.42) and oboe (0.39); rate correlated with loudness for cello (0.40) and theremin (0.48). Rate and extent negatively correlated for cello (−0.33). Metrical stress affected rate for violin and cello; for violin, structure explained half the variance in extent (R² 0.49). Short notes got little vibrato and smaller extent. Earlier work cited there: short notes show only an upper arch of the pitch movement (Castellengo et al. 1989); notes end moving towards the next one (Sundberg 1979).

Ornoy and Cohen 2018, "Analysis of contemporary violin recordings of 19th century repertoire: identifying trends and impacts", Frontiers in Psychology 9:2233. Twenty recordings, 1999–2017, Beethoven Op. 30 No. 1 and Brahms Op. 78. Most players oscillate at about 5 to 6 Hz. Vibrato widely used but omitted in places by many players; some use slow wide vibrato in quiet contemplative passages and fast narrow vibrato in dramatic ones, others none in quiet low passages. Onset sometimes delayed on a phrase's first note. Small sample; the authors call the conclusions tentative.

## The instrument's part in it

Fritz, Woodhouse, Cheng, Cross, Blackwell and Moore 2010, "Perceptual studies of violin body damping and vibrato", JASA 127(1):513–524. A vibrato moves the partials across the body's resonances, so the body turns it into fluctuations of amplitude and spectrum as well as pitch. A physical model with a measured body produces this by itself; only the finger's motion has to be supplied.

## When players use it: the historical record

Qualitative, and argued over. Geminiani 1751 recommends the "close shake" as often as possible, and describes swelling it on long notes. Leopold Mozart 1756 condemns vibrato on every note and keeps it for sustained notes and phrase ends as an ornament. Early recordings: Joachim uses noticeable vibrato only occasionally, for emphasis; Kreisler's from 1904 on is continuous; continuous vibrato became normal in the 1920s and 1930s (CHARM, "The changing sound of music", chapter 5; The Strad). How sparingly earlier players actually used it is disputed.
